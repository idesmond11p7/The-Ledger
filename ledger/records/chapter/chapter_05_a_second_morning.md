Chapter Five — A Second Morning
“What the fuck?”

I stared at the mirror.

For several seconds, I couldn't make myself look away.

The person standing in front of me was still there, breathing when I breathed, shifting when I shifted. The reflection wasn't distorted. There was no strange light in the glass, no dreamlike haze, nothing that could explain what I was seeing.

It was just a mirror.

And somehow that made it worse.

I turned my head toward the room.

The bedroom looked exactly as it had a moment ago.

Morning light had begun to filter through the curtains, though I hadn't noticed it until then. It fell across the wooden floor in pale rectangles, catching the edges of the furniture and leaving the corners in softer shadow. The walls were a muted cream colour that changed subtly depending on where the sunlight touched them. A desk sat beneath the window, a chair tucked underneath it. There was a wardrobe against the opposite wall and a narrow shelf crowded with books.

Nothing about it looked supernatural.

That bothered me.

I looked toward the window.

The curtains moved slightly from the air coming through the gap. Beyond them I could see the tops of buildings and a strip of pale sky. A road ran somewhere below. I could hear a car passing, followed by the muffled sound of somebody talking.

Normal.

Everything sounded normal.

I looked back at the mirror.

The stranger was still looking at me.

I stepped closer.

He did the same.

My heart was beating too quickly.

I raised my right hand and slowly touched my cheek.

The reflection did exactly the same.

My fingers met warm skin.

I pulled them away.

Then I touched my face again, more carefully this time.

The shape was wrong.

Not wrong in the sense that anything was physically malformed. It was simply not my face.

The jaw was narrower than mine had been. The cheeks were softer. The eyes were much larger, framed by dark lashes that looked ridiculous when I remembered my own face.

And the hair—

Jesus Christ.

It was long.

Far too long.

Black at first glance, but with dark teal running through it and brighter green-blue colour underneath whenever the light caught it. It fell past my shoulders and continued down my back, thick enough that I couldn't understand how anybody was supposed to manage it every morning.

I reached behind myself and gathered some of it in one hand.

It was real.

I let it fall.

The weight of it brushed against my back.

I looked down at the clothes I was wearing.

They were unfamiliar too. Loose enough to hide most of my shape, but they couldn't hide the fact that the body itself was much smaller and more delicate than mine had been.

I looked around the room again.

There was a bed behind me.

A pair of shoes near it.

A school bag on the floor.

Several books on the desk.

A folded shirt.

A glass of water beside the bed.

Ordinary things.

Someone lived here.

Someone had been living here.

Someone whose room I was standing in.

The thought made my stomach turn.

I walked over to the desk.

There were notebooks stacked beside a few textbooks, all written in Japanese. I recognized enough of the writing to know what it was, but not enough to understand everything at a glance.

I picked one up.

The handwriting was neat.

Very neat.

I flipped through several pages.

Schoolwork.

Notes.

Dates.

Nothing immediately useful.

I put it back.

My eyes moved around the room again.

There was no photograph.

No family picture.

No obvious name written anywhere.

Nothing that immediately told me who this person was.

I checked the wardrobe.

Clothes.

Most of them looked expensive without being flashy. Everything was arranged more neatly than I would have expected from a teenager.

I opened a drawer.

Nothing.

Another.

Still nothing.

I stood there for a while.

My hands were beginning to shake.

I looked at them.

They were not my hands.

That thought landed differently this time.

Not abstractly.

Not like the mirror.

These were literally my hands now.

I flexed my fingers.

The movement felt natural.

Too natural.

That scared me more than if they had felt foreign.

I walked back toward the mirror.

“Okay.”

My voice came out soft.

I froze.

I tried again.

“Okay.”

The same voice.

It wasn't merely higher than mine.

It was genuinely soft. Feminine enough that if I'd heard it from another room, I wouldn't have assumed it belonged to a boy.

I stared at the mirror.

“No.”

My voice answered me from the room.

I swallowed.

“No, no, no.”

I sounded like somebody else.

I pressed both hands against my face.

This couldn't be happening.

I had died.

I remembered that much.

I remembered the feeling of everything disappearing.

I remembered the strange place afterward.

The stars.

The impossible spaces.

The feeling of moving through something far larger than anything I could understand.

And then this.

A room.

A body.

A life that wasn't mine.

I lowered my hands.

The mirror waited.

I thought about calling someone.

Then I realized I didn't know anyone here.

I thought about leaving.

Then I realized I didn't know where I was.

I thought about going back.

There was nowhere to go back to.

That was the thought that finally broke through everything else.

My old room was gone.

My house was gone.

My parents were gone.

My friends were gone.

School was gone.

Everything I'd complained about for years was suddenly something I couldn't have back.

The realization hit so hard that I had to sit down.

I sat on the edge of the bed and stared at the floor.

For a while, I didn't think anything.

Then everything came at once.

What had happened?

Was I dead?

Was this some kind of afterlife?

Was I dreaming?

If I was dreaming, why could I feel the floor beneath my feet?

If I was dead, why did I still remember everything?

If I wasn't dead, where was I?

Who was this person?

Why was I in his body?

Why did the room look like somebody else's life?

Why did I have no idea what was going on?

What happened to my parents?

Would they know I was dead?

Would they think I'd run away?

Would somebody find me?

Would there even be a body?

I squeezed my eyes shut.

No.

Don't think about that.

Think about something else.

Anything else.

The room.

The desk.

The window.

The books.

The—

My old school.

I remembered sitting there.

I remembered people complaining about teachers.

I remembered the stupid things Daniel used to say.

I remembered my mother's voice from the kitchen.

I remembered my phone.

My bed.

My clothes.

The stupid crack in the ceiling.

All those ordinary things I'd never thought I'd miss.

I opened my eyes.

The room was still here.

The sun had climbed higher.

Morning was continuing without me.

People were going to work.

Students were probably going to school.

Somebody was probably already late.

The world had continued.

Mine hadn't.

I looked down at my hands again.

For some reason, that made me want to cry.

I didn't at first.

I just sat there.

Then my eyes started burning.

I rubbed them.

That only made it worse.

“Come on.”

My voice cracked.

I laughed once.

It wasn't funny.

I covered my face.

The tears came properly after that.

I tried to stop them.

I couldn't.

I cried because I didn't know where I was.

I cried because I didn't know whose body I was wearing.

I cried because I remembered being seventeen and thinking I still had plenty of time to figure things out.

I cried because there were so many stupid things I'd complained about that suddenly seemed unbelievably valuable.

I would have taken another boring morning.

Another argument.

Another embarrassing day at school.

Another night lying awake with my phone beside me.

I would have taken all of it.

I would have taken the parts of my life I'd hated if it meant I could have my life back.

At some point I lay down.

I don't remember exactly when I stopped crying.

I remember the room becoming darker.

I remember pulling the blanket around myself.

I remember thinking that tomorrow I would figure something out.

That thought was probably the only thing keeping me conscious.

Tomorrow.

I'd figure it out tomorrow.

I had to.

Eventually, exhaustion won.

I slept.

When I woke again, sunlight was already filling the room.

For a moment I didn't remember.

Then I opened my eyes.

The ceiling was unfamiliar.

I stared at it.

Then I remembered everything.

I closed my eyes again.

“Right.”

My voice was still wrong.

I opened them.

The morning looked different from the night.

The room no longer felt like a strange place I'd accidentally wandered into. Sunlight reached almost everywhere now, revealing small details I'd missed before. The walls had a warm cream tone. The wooden floor had faint scratches near the desk and darker marks where furniture had been moved. Dust showed itself in the light near the window, drifting lazily through the air.

There was something strangely comforting about that.

It was just a room.

A very unfamiliar room.

But a room.

I sat up.

My hair fell over my shoulder and nearly into my face.

I grabbed it.

“Oh, come on.”

I pulled it back and looked at the enormous amount of hair gathered in my hands.

Who had this much hair?

I stood up.

My legs felt slightly different from what I was used to. Lighter. The balance was different too, though I couldn't explain exactly how.

I took a few steps around the room.

My body adjusted quickly.

That was unsettling.

I wasn't consciously remembering how to walk.

It already knew.

I stopped.

My body knew things I didn't.

I didn't know what that meant.

I decided not to think about it yet.

The bathroom was across the hall.

I found it after opening the wrong door first.

That seemed about right.

Inside, everything was clean and ordinary. White tile, a small sink, a mirror above it, towels folded neatly beside the shower. Morning light came through the frosted window and turned the room almost golden.

I stared at my reflection again.

There was no darkness to hide behind now.

I looked like a girl.

Not exactly.

But enough that if somebody saw me from a distance, I wasn't sure they'd know.

I leaned closer.

The face was almost delicate.

Large green eyes.

Fine eyebrows.

Long lashes.

A small nose.

Soft lips.

Pale skin with a warm flush through the cheeks.

I touched my face.

“Seriously?”

I turned sideways.

That did not help.

The body was extremely slender. Narrow shoulders. Thin arms. Small waist. Long legs.

Nothing about it looked masculine in the way I was used to seeing myself.

I looked down.

Then back at the mirror.

I was male.

I stared for several seconds.

“...Oh.”

I looked again.

Then away.

Then back.

The fact hadn't changed.

I rubbed my face.

This was ridiculous.

I'd spent the entire night trying to understand how I'd ended up in another person's body, and somehow I'd completely avoided dealing with the obvious part.

I wasn't in my body.

This body was male.

It just happened to look like this.

I stared at myself again.

For a brief moment, irritation washed over me.

Why did I have to look like this?

Why couldn't I have woken up looking normal?

Why did I have to deal with people mistaking me for a girl on top of everything else?

I ran a hand through my hair.

Then stopped.

The hair was actually beautiful.

That annoyed me.

I looked at my reflection.

The eyes were beautiful too.

The face was beautiful.

The whole body was...

Elegant.

I hated that I couldn't argue with it.

I looked down again.

“Wait.”

I stared.

Then looked at the mirror.

Then down again.

“...I have a dick.”

I stood there for another moment.

For some reason, that was reassuring.

At least I hadn't woken up as somebody completely different in every possible sense.

I was still a guy.

I just looked nothing like one.

I covered my face.

“Damn.”

I dropped my hands.

“Damn. I got reincarnated as a fucking femboy.”

I waited for the universe to punish me for saying it.

Nothing happened.

I looked back at the mirror.

“At least I look elegant.”

I immediately felt stupid.

Then I almost laughed.

Almost.

The tension in my chest loosened slightly.

That mattered.

I turned toward the shower.

I needed to wash.

After everything that had happened, taking a shower was probably the most normal thing I could do.

The water was warm.

Steam gathered slowly against the mirror while I stood beneath it, and for the first time since waking up, I wasn't trying to solve my entire existence.

I just stood there.

Water ran through my hair and down my shoulders.

I closed my eyes.

The old life came back.

Not as sharply as the night before.

Just pieces.

My room.

My school.

People I knew.

Things I had wanted to do.

Things I'd been angry about.

Things I'd planned to fix.

Later.

That word felt strange now.

I'd spent so much time assuming there would always be one.

Another day.

Another week.

Another chance.

Maybe this was one.

The thought came quietly.

I opened my eyes.

Steam blurred the room around me.

Maybe this was a second chance.

I didn't know why.

I didn't know who had given it to me.

I didn't even know if it was a gift.

But I was alive.

Whatever else was true, I was alive.

And if I was alive, then maybe I could do something with that.

The thought didn't erase everything.

I still missed home.

I still didn't understand what had happened.

I still didn't know whose life I had fallen into.

But for the first time since the mirror, those questions didn't feel like the only things in the world.

I turned the water off.

I dried myself, got dressed, and went back into the bedroom.

The morning felt different.

Not happier.

Just possible.

Finding information turned out to be easier once I stopped looking for some grand explanation.

The apartment had plenty of ordinary evidence.

A wallet was in the top drawer of the desk.

Inside were several bills, coins, a few cards and an identification document.

I pulled it out.

The name printed on it stopped me.

Ira Lowe.

I read it again.

Ira Lowe.

That was the name of the person whose life I had apparently stepped into.

I sat down.

“Ira.”

The name felt strange in my head.

I tried saying it.

“Ira Lowe.”

My voice made the name sound softer than I expected.

I looked at the document again.

There was an age.

Sixteen.

I stared.

“Sixteen?”

That explained some things.

Not everything.

But enough.

The body was younger than I was.

Or had been.

I put the document down.

Nothing happened.

No childhood memories appeared.

No faces.

No voice whispered inside my head.

There was just the name.

Ira Lowe.

That was all I had.

I searched through the desk more carefully.

There were school papers.

Letters.

A timetable.

A few textbooks.

Eventually I found an envelope with a school crest printed on the front.

I opened it.

The letter inside was an admission notice.

I read it twice.

Then a third time.

Kuoh Academy.

I sat back.

Kuoh.

I'd never heard of it.

There was a date near the bottom.

The entrance examination was tomorrow.

I stared at the page.

“Tomorrow?”

I checked the date again.

Tomorrow.

I looked around the room.

Then back at the letter.

“So I'm sixteen, apparently named Ira Lowe, living in an apartment I don't remember, and I'm supposed to take an entrance exam tomorrow.”

I leaned back in the chair.

“Great.”

At least this was a problem I understood.

School.

School had rules.

Tests had questions.

Questions had answers.

I could work with that.

The problem was getting there.

The address on the letter wasn't close enough to walk.

I found a printed map folded inside one of the drawers.

It was old-fashioned by the standards I remembered, but it worked. Streets, railway lines, districts and city names were printed clearly across the paper.

I traced the route with my finger.

There was a train station within walking distance.

From there, I could get toward Kuoh.

I checked the money in the wallet.

Enough.

Probably.

I hoped.

Then another thought occurred to me.

I didn't even know where the station was.

The first part of the journey was surprisingly normal.

I locked the apartment behind me and stood outside for a moment.

The street was already awake.

Cars moved through the intersection at the end of the road. Bicycles slipped between pedestrians. Shop shutters were being lifted one by one. A delivery truck was parked halfway across the street while two men stood beside it, apparently disagreeing about where something was supposed to go.

The morning smelled like warm pavement, food and faint exhaust.

Somewhere nearby, oil crackled in a pan.

A bicycle bell rang.

A radio played from inside one of the shops.

The sunlight reflected off windows and car roofs, throwing little flashes across the street whenever something moved.

It was busy without being chaotic.

I walked with the map folded in one hand.

Every few minutes I checked it.

Then the street.

Then the map again.

The first time, I thought I was doing well.

The second time, I realized I was going in the wrong direction.

I stopped beside a vending machine and unfolded the map.

The station was supposed to be southeast.

I looked down the road.

Nothing about it looked southeast.

I turned the map.

That didn't help.

I turned it again.

Still nothing.

“Okay.”

I looked around.

A woman carrying two shopping bags was approaching from the opposite direction.

She noticed the map.

“You trying to find the station?”

I looked up.

She was an adult, probably somewhere in her twenties, with short brown hair and a yellow cardigan over a white blouse. She looked like she'd been out shopping rather than heading anywhere in particular.

“Yeah.”

She glanced at the map.

“Which station?”

I showed her.

She leaned closer.

“Oh. That's a bit back.”

“Of course it is.”

She smiled.

“You came from that way?”

“Apparently.”

“Then you walked past it.”

I looked over my shoulder.

“How far?”

“Not very. Maybe five minutes.”

I sighed.

“Okay. Thanks.”

“No problem.”

She shifted the shopping bags in her hands.

“Just go back to the main road. There's a pharmacy on the corner. The station entrance is just after that.”

I nodded.

“Got it.”

She started walking again, then stopped.

“Actually, wait.”

I looked back.

“You're going the other direction.”

I stared at her.

She laughed.

“Sorry.”

“It's fine.”

She pointed behind me.

“That way.”

“Right.”

“Good luck.”

“Thanks.”

She continued down the road.

I waited until she was gone before looking at my map again.

“Fantastic.”

I turned around.

At least I knew where I was going now.

Mostly.

The station was larger than I expected.

It had several platforms, a broad entrance and enough people moving through it that I had to stop for a moment just to understand where I was supposed to go.

Announcements played over the speakers.

People talked over them.

A train arrived with a metallic rush, brakes squealing briefly before the doors opened.

A group of students hurried down the stairs.

Two office workers stood near a pillar drinking coffee from paper cups.

Someone was arguing with a ticket machine.

I wasn't sure how they had managed to start an argument with a machine, but somehow they had.

I bought my ticket after asking the clerk twice where I needed to go.

The clerk looked at me strangely.

I couldn't tell whether it was because of my voice, my appearance, or the fact that I looked completely lost.

Probably some combination of all three.

The train arrived a few minutes later.

I got on.

The carriage was crowded enough that I had to stand near the door.

I held the map against my chest.

As the train moved, the city slid past the windows.

Buildings gave way to smaller streets.

Smaller streets became wider roads.

Then trees.

Then more buildings.

The landscape changed gradually enough that I could watch it happen.

Ordinary houses with small gardens.

Apartment blocks packed closely together.

Convenience stores.

School buildings.

Telephone poles.

Narrow roads filled with bicycles.

Every so often the train passed a stretch of green where the city seemed to loosen its grip for a few minutes.

I found myself watching everything.

Sunlight on the windows.

Reflections of passengers.

The wires running alongside the railway.

Clouds moving across the sky.

Even the little scratches in the glass.

It was strange how beautiful ordinary things could look when you knew you'd lost the life that contained them.

I looked down at my hands.

For a moment, the old sadness returned.

I missed home.

I missed people I couldn't even call anymore.

I missed being able to complain about things that now seemed unbelievably small.

The thought sat there.

Then the train entered a tunnel.

The window became a dark mirror.

My new face appeared again.

I stared at it.

“Right.”

I looked away.

Not today.

I wasn't going to spend the entire day breaking apart.

The train emerged into sunlight.

I looked out again.

Kuoh was bigger than I'd expected.

The station alone gave me that impression.

There were more people than I had anticipated, more streets, more shops, more buildings and more traffic. It wasn't some tiny town where everyone knew everyone else.

It felt like an actual city.

A lively one.

I stepped out of the station and immediately realized I had no idea which direction to walk.

I unfolded the map.

A man nearly walked into me.

“Sorry.”

“Sorry.”

We both moved aside.

He was wearing a dark suit and carrying a briefcase, his black hair neatly combed back. He gave me a distracted nod and disappeared into the crowd before I could say anything else.

I looked at the map.

The streets around the station were dense.

I followed the route.

For ten minutes.

Then fifteen.

Then I realized I'd somehow crossed the same intersection twice.

I stopped.

“No.”

I looked behind me.

Then ahead.

Then at the map.

“This is the same street.”

A bicycle bell rang behind me.

I stepped aside.

A teenage girl rode past, her dark ponytail swinging behind her. She braked a few metres away and glanced back.

“You okay?”

“Yeah.”

She looked at the map in my hand.

“You looking for somewhere?”

“The academy.”

She nodded.

“Oh.”

She looked down the street.

“You went past the turn.”

I stared at the map.

“Again?”

She laughed.

“Yeah. Again.”

I looked at her.

She shrugged.

“It's easy to miss. The road bends.”

“Apparently I'm really good at this.”

“You're not.”

I stared at her.

She grinned.

“Sorry.”

I laughed despite myself.

“It's fine.”

She pointed farther down the road.

“Go back to that intersection. There's a bakery on the corner. Turn there and keep going.”

I looked where she was pointing.

“Then I'll get there?”

“Eventually.”

She said it casually, not as a joke.

I stared at her for a second.

“That's not very reassuring.”

She laughed.

“You'll be fine.”

Then she pushed off again.

“Thanks.”

She lifted one hand without turning around.

“Good luck.”

I watched her disappear into the crowd.

I looked at my map.

Maybe I needed a better map.

Or a better brain.

Possibly both.

Still, the city was starting to grow on me.

There was something different about the atmosphere here.

People were more expressive.

Not necessarily kinder.

Not necessarily friendlier.

Just... more.

A couple argued outside a café, neither particularly concerned about who could hear them. An elderly man sat nearby reading a newspaper and pretending not to listen. Two children ran past a shop window chasing each other while their mother shouted after them. A woman leaned out of an upstairs window to call something across the street.

A group of young adults passed in conversation, laughing loudly enough that people turned to look at them.

Even the way people dressed seemed slightly more striking.

There were plenty of ordinary clothes, but there was also an odd abundance of people who looked unusually polished, unusually attractive, or simply more physically exaggerated than I was used to seeing in everyday life. Tall people looked taller. Slender people looked almost unnaturally graceful. Women with naturally striking figures seemed to be everywhere, moving through the streets as though the city had somehow decided that ordinary proportions were merely a suggestion.

I caught myself staring once.

I looked away.

Okay.

So Kuoh was weird.

Not supernatural-weird.

Just...

Weird.

I kept walking.

By the time I reached the road leading toward the academy, the sun was higher.

The streets widened.

Buildings became more substantial.

There were more students around now.

Uniforms everywhere.

Some walked in groups.

Others rode bicycles.

A few hurried along with books pressed against their chests.

I passed a convenience store and stopped long enough to buy something to drink.

The cashier looked at me twice.

I couldn't blame him.

Long black-and-green hair.

Pale skin.

Slender frame.

A face that made it difficult to decide what I was supposed to be at a glance.

I paid and stepped outside.

A gust of wind caught my hair and sent half of it across my face.

I grabbed it.

“Seriously?”

A passing student laughed.

I looked over.

She immediately looked away, pretending she hadn't.

I shook my head and kept walking.

The closer I got to the academy, the stranger the city seemed.

Not frightening.

If anything, the opposite.

It felt almost too lively.

A group of students burst into laughter at the crossing.

Someone nearly dropped an entire stack of books and caught them against her chest at the last second.

Two boys were arguing over something that appeared to be completely pointless.

A teacher shouted after them.

A bicycle went past with a basket full of flowers.

A woman carrying groceries stopped in the middle of the pavement to talk to a friend, forcing half a dozen people to weave around them.

Nothing extraordinary was happening.

And yet everything seemed to have slightly more energy than it should.

I couldn't explain it.

I wasn't sure I wanted to.

For once, I was enjoying not knowing.

Then I saw the gates.

I stopped.

Kuoh Academy stood beyond them.

It was much larger than I'd imagined from the letter.

The grounds stretched far enough that I couldn't see the entire campus from the road. The main building rose beyond the entrance, surrounded by trees and carefully maintained paths. Other structures stood farther back, partly hidden behind greenery.

The architecture was elegant without looking old-fashioned.

Stone.

Glass.

Dark roofing.

Large windows catching the afternoon light.

Students moved through the grounds in groups, their voices carrying faintly toward the road.

The entire place looked expensive.

Not merely expensive in the way a nice school looked expensive.

Established.

Like it had been there long enough to develop its own gravity.

I stood there with the map still in my hand.

For the first time since waking up, I wasn't thinking about death.

I wasn't thinking about my old life.

I wasn't thinking about the mirror.

I was just looking.

Tomorrow I'd have to walk through those gates.

Tomorrow I'd take an entrance examination for a school I'd never heard of, in a city I'd only discovered that morning, while living in a body belonging to someone named Ira Lowe whose life I knew absolutely nothing about.

I looked down at the letter.

Then back at the academy.

I let out a slow breath.

“Well.”

A breeze moved through the trees.

Somewhere inside the campus, a bell rang.

Students began moving between buildings.

For a moment, sunlight caught the windows all at once, turning the upper floor bright enough that I had to squint.

I smiled despite myself.

This was insane.

Completely insane.

But it was also the first thing that had felt like a beginning.

I folded the map and put it away.

Tomorrow, I would take the exam.

Today, I had finally found somewhere to go.
