Chapter 8
The Architecture of Waiting
For the first time since morning, I had nothing I needed to do.

The kettle was still heating.

I sat at the table with my hands resting beside the brown envelope, listening to the faint, uneven sound coming from the kitchen. Nothing in the apartment had changed since I had returned. The table was where I had left it. The light above the counter still cast its pale rectangle across the worktop. Beyond the window, the evening had settled between the neighboring buildings, leaving their walls a flat, dark grey.

I had spent the day trying to get somewhere. First to the academy, then through the examination, then back home with the vague satisfaction of having completed something I could point to and call progress. Now there was nowhere else I had to be. No train to catch. No form to submit. No unfamiliar corridor to navigate.

The silence ought to have been a relief.

Instead, it left me alone with the envelope.

I pulled it closer.

The blue examination placard marked 1044 lay on top of the other papers. I turned it over, although I already knew what was on the back, then placed it beside the envelope. The application material beneath it was neatly arranged, the pages aligned as if someone had taken care to make the whole thing look reassuringly ordinary.

That was the problem with it.

It looked ordinary.

I could point to the stamps, the printed categories, the formal wording, and the contact information. I could tell someone that I had sat an entrance examination at Kuoh Academy because the paperwork proved I had. If they asked for evidence, I could put the documents on the table and let them read.

But if they asked me where I had gone to school before this, I had nothing.

Not nothing on paper. There were entries, classifications, and course equivalencies. An educational history had been assembled for me under the heading Overseas Transfer / Independent Curriculum. The words sat there with the confident, impersonal neatness of an answer that had already survived someone else's scrutiny.

I read them again.

Overseas Transfer. Independent Curriculum.

It sounded like the sort of explanation an adult could deliver without thinking about it. A child had studied abroad, circumstances had changed, and now the child was transferring into a Japanese school. There would be records. There would be an explanation for the gaps. If anyone needed more information, they could contact the institution responsible.

Except I was the child.

I was the one who would have to stand there when someone asked what the school had been like.

What subjects had I studied? Who had taught me? What did I do after class? Had I made friends? What had I liked about the place?

I could invent an answer to any one of those questions. Probably. I could say something vague about studying independently, keep my eyes steady, and hope the person asking was too busy to care. But a conversation did not stop because I had run out of prepared sentences. People asked another question. They followed the first answer with something small and ordinary, and suddenly the story you had constructed began to wobble.

At the academy, I had managed it by saying that everything was in the dossier.

I glanced at the envelope.

That answer had worked because the staff member had accepted it and moved on. It did not mean I could live inside the explanation indefinitely.

My thumb pressed against the edge of the placard. The card bent slightly before I loosened my grip.

What am I supposed to do if somebody actually wants to know me?

The question was embarrassing in a way I could not quite explain. I had bigger problems. I was living in a world I had not chosen, in a body that still demanded more attention than I wanted to give it, under a name that belonged to someone whose life I could not remember living. There were questions about money, housing, school, and the fact that I apparently had no legal guardian to call when an institution decided I needed one.

And here I was, worrying about what to say if somebody asked me about my school days.

I put the placard down.

The kettle continued heating. A small sound came from the kitchen as the metal shifted with the rising temperature.

For a moment, the noise reminded me of home.

Not in some dramatic, cinematic way. I did not see a perfect picture of my old kitchen, and I did not hear a voice calling my name from another room. I remembered an ordinary evening, the sort that had been so unremarkable I would never have thought to preserve it. The expectation that if I wanted something, I could ask. That if something strange happened, there would be someone I could tell. My mother might be busy, tired, distracted, or in the middle of something else, but she was there. The possibility of talking to her had been built into the shape of the day.

I stared at the kettle.

I could almost begin a sentence.

You won't believe what happened to me.

Then the sentence stopped being funny.

There was no conversation waiting for me on the other side of it. No way to explain that I had become someone else without sounding insane, no way to ask what I should do next, no way to find out whether the person I had been was still expected to come home.

My mother was alive. Somewhere in the life I had left, the world had continued without the explanation I wanted to give it. That made the distance harder to fit inside my head, not easier. There was no clean ending I could accept and no ordinary route back.

I rubbed my face with both hands, careful of my hair where it had fallen across my fingers.

"Okay," I muttered.

Nothing answered.

The word sounded stupid in the empty room, but I kept going because stopping meant returning to the papers.

"Okay. Fine."

I sat straighter and looked at the documents again. If I could not explain the past, I could at least learn what had been written about it. If I could not make this world less strange, I could stop making the parts I understood harder than they needed to be.

That sounded reasonable.

It also sounded suspiciously like something I was telling myself because I needed the evening to have a point.

I had always been good at deciding that I would do better next time. The decision itself was easy. It arrived with a little burst of energy, gave me something to imagine, and made the distance between where I was and where I wanted to be feel temporarily manageable. The difficult part came later, when the feeling wore off and the next small inconvenience appeared.

Maybe that was why I wanted a rule.

Something simple enough that I could remember it when I was tired. Something I could do even when I did not understand what was happening.

Think ahead. Pay attention. Stop walking into things unprepared.

If I could anticipate what people wanted, what they might ask, what could go wrong, then perhaps I could avoid being caught with nothing to say. I could prepare for school, learn the routines, make fewer mistakes. I could become the sort of person who did not have to improvise every time the world demanded something from him.

The idea took hold faster than it deserved to.

I could feel myself wanting it to be true. That was not the same as knowing it would work, but I did not particularly want to examine the difference.

I had no control over how I had ended up here. I did not know why this body was mine, why these documents existed, or how much of the life called Ira Lowe had been assembled before I arrived. I could spend the entire night staring at those questions and still wake up with the same problems.

But I could prepare.

I could stop being careless.

I could take this seriously enough that, when something happened, I would have at least one idea what to do.

It was an unfair rule. If I failed to predict something, I could blame myself for not thinking hard enough. If another person behaved in a way I had not expected, I could tell myself I should have considered it. The rule promised control by making every surprise feel like a personal failure.

I did not see that part clearly.

What I saw was a way forward.

I had made it through the day. I had travelled to an unfamiliar school, completed an examination, answered questions, and returned to an apartment where I could put my belongings down without having to ask anyone's permission. I was still frightened. I was still confused. But the day had happened, and I had participated in it.

That counted for something.

I gathered the pages into a stack and returned them to the envelope. The examination placard went on top. I tucked the flap closed, then opened it again to check that I had not left anything underneath.

Nothing was missing.

I checked once more anyway.

The kettle had finished heating. I went to the kitchen, made myself something to drink, and carried the cup back to the table. The warmth travelled through the ceramic into my fingers. I took a cautious sip and immediately regretted not waiting longer.

"Brilliant."

I set it down and blew across the surface.

The apartment still felt too quiet. The walls seemed closer when I stayed in one place for too long, and the paperwork had begun to feel like an audience waiting for me to say something intelligent. I did not have anything intelligent to say. I wanted air, a little food, and a reason to stop looking at the same four walls.

I could go for a walk.

That was all. No grand plan. No new life philosophy to test against the population of Kuoh. Just a walk to somewhere I could buy a drink or something small to eat, then back home before the evening got too late.

I put the dead silver Panasonic flip phone in my pocket out of habit. It was still dead, its screen offering no help whatsoever. I checked my wallet, counted the cash without taking every note out, and slipped it into my coat.

The dossier stayed on the table.

Before leaving, I glanced at it once more.

Everything I needed to prove that I was Ira Lowe was inside that envelope. Almost nothing inside it could help me explain what being Ira Lowe was supposed to mean.

I turned away before I could start reading it again.

The lock clicked behind me, and the sound carried down the apartment corridor. The air outside my door smelled faintly of detergent and somebody's dinner. A television murmured behind a wall, the voices too muffled to make out. I descended the stairs, passed through the building entrance, and stepped into the evening.

The residential lane had already begun to darken. The buildings stood close enough together that the last light reached the upper walls more easily than the pavement. Overhead wires crossed between utility poles, their lines cutting across narrow pieces of sky. A bicycle leaned against a wall beside a doorway. Someone had left a pair of sandals outside an apartment, toes pointed in opposite directions.

I walked past them.

There was no reason to hurry. I had nowhere particular to be, which meant I kept looking at the things I would normally have ignored: a shutter being pulled down over a shop window, a woman carrying a plastic bag against her hip, a man checking the contents of a crate before moving it inside. The street was not doing anything for my benefit. People were finishing their day, getting home, buying things they needed, and making small decisions that had nothing to do with me.

That was oddly reassuring.

At the corner, the lane opened onto a more commercial street. The change was gradual but clear. There were more signs, more light spilling from windows, more people moving in both directions. A bicycle passed close enough that I heard its tires whisper over a rough patch in the pavement. The rider called something over his shoulder to someone farther behind him, and the words disappeared beneath the traffic.

I slowed to look at the shopfronts.

A small café stood between a shop selling household goods and a narrow storefront with its shutters already down. Its wooden door was set back from the pavement, and a modest sign hung above the window. The light inside was warmer than the street lighting, but not bright enough to make the room look inviting from a distance. A few tables were visible through the glass. Someone sat near the window with a newspaper spread open in front of him.

I was not particularly hungry, but the place looked quiet, and I could buy something without having to navigate a crowded counter.

That was a good enough reason.

I opened the door.

A small bell sounded above it. The noise of the street came in with me, then diminished when the door swung shut. Inside, the café smelled of coffee, old wood, and the faint stale trace of tobacco. The counter ran along one side of the room. Behind it, a woman was wiping a cup with a cloth, her attention divided between the work in her hands and the regular sitting near the window.

The man had a newspaper open, one finger holding his place on the page. Every time the bell above the door moved, his eyes lifted toward the entrance. After a brief look, he returned to the same patch of print.

I noticed him because I was trying to work out where I should stand.

The woman behind the counter looked up.

"Good evening."

"Good evening," I replied.

My Japanese came out naturally. The words were familiar even when the room was not, and for a moment I felt an uncomplicated flicker of relief.

Then I realized I had stopped just inside the door.

The woman waited. Her gaze moved from my face to the empty tables and back again. I could not tell whether she was waiting for me to choose a seat or trying to decide if I needed help.

I had been standing there long enough to make either interpretation reasonable.

Think ahead.

The rule came back to me because there was suddenly something to think about. I looked at the menu displayed near the counter, then at the woman's expression. She had noticed my hesitation. If she thought I was lost, she might ask whether I needed help or whether I understood the menu. If I answered before she had to repeat herself, I could avoid making the moment more awkward.

That was the theory, anyway.

"Are you looking for something?" she asked. "Or do you need help with the menu?"

There it was.

I felt a small, ridiculous surge of satisfaction. I had anticipated the question. It was not difficult, exactly, but I had managed to notice what was happening before it happened.

"No, thank you," I said. "I can read Japanese. I was only deciding what to order."

The woman blinked once, then smiled.

"All right. You can sit wherever you like."

"Thank you for your consideration."

The smile widened.

I knew it was the wrong phrase as soon as I heard myself say it. Nothing in her face suggested that she had been offended. If anything, she looked as though she was trying not to laugh.

"You don't have to speak to me like I'm a school principal," she said. "I'm just asking what you want to drink."

Heat crept into my cheeks.

"I didn't mean—"

"I know." She put the cup down and gestured toward the tables. "Sit down first. You can take your time."

I moved toward a small table near the wall, choosing the chair that gave me a clear view of the counter and the door. The chair legs scraped against the floor when I pulled it out. I sat, arranged my coat beside me, and tried not to look as if I had just lost an argument with a cup of coffee.

The woman returned to wiping the counter.

So much for my brilliant new method.

I had predicted the question. That part had worked. But I had not predicted my own response, or the fact that a perfectly harmless exchange could leave me wishing I had said about six fewer words. I had been so focused on avoiding the wrong answer that I had managed to make an ordinary answer sound like a formal complaint.

Still, I had got the question right.

I held on to that small victory while I studied the menu.

The options were simple enough. Coffee, tea, a few soft drinks, and some light food. I chose a drink and something to eat, then stood and went to the counter to place the order. This time I kept my request short. The woman took it without comment and returned to the machine behind her.

The regular turned a page.

I went back to my table and rested my hands on the edge while I waited. From here, I could hear the low hiss of the coffee machine and the small clink of ceramic as the woman moved cups around behind the counter. Beyond the window, people passed in fragments: a sleeve, a bag, the wheel of a bicycle, a face turned toward a shop sign. Each time the door opened, the street became briefly louder before the sound was shut outside again.

The man near the window lifted his eyes toward the entrance when someone passed. The person kept walking. He looked down at the newspaper and found his place.

I wondered what he was reading.

Then I wondered why I cared.

The woman set my drink and food on the counter, and I collected them. The cup was warmer than I expected. I carried it carefully, aware of how easily I could spill it if I turned too quickly, and placed it on the table before sitting again.

For a while, nothing happened.

I drank. I ate. The food was ordinary, which was exactly what I wanted from it. The first few mouthfuls quieted the hollow feeling in my stomach, and the heat of the drink loosened some of the tension I had been carrying in my shoulders. I did not have to understand the café. I did not have to learn anyone's history or guess what the man with the newspaper was thinking. I could simply sit there and finish what I had ordered.

The bell rang again.

Two teenagers entered, talking over each other. One was carrying a folded sheet of paper; the other kept glancing past him toward the street, as if the person they were looking for might appear if he checked often enough. They were about my age, perhaps a little older. Neither seemed especially concerned about being in the café. They greeted the woman behind the counter, received a casual reply, and moved toward the counter to ask something about the time.

I looked back at my drink.

I did not want to stare. I also could not help noticing when one of them glanced at me.

His eyes passed over my face, then dropped toward my hair, which had fallen forward over one shoulder. He leaned toward his friend and said something too quietly for me to hear. The friend looked at me, then back at him.

"That's a boy," the second one said, in the matter-of-fact tone of someone correcting a minor mistake.

"I know that now."

"Then why did you—"

"I only saw the hair."

They were not whispering particularly well.

My fingers tightened around the cup.

For half a second, I wanted to be offended. I could already feel the familiar defensive response arranging itself: they were laughing at me, they had decided I was strange, and I should act as if I did not care. It was a neat interpretation. It also required me to assume more than I had actually heard.

The first boy looked over again.

"Sorry," he said, not sounding especially distressed. "From behind, I thought you were a girl."

"That's fine," I replied.

It came out too quickly.

His friend gave a short laugh, but the first boy had already returned to the paper in his hand. They were trying to work out where to meet someone, arguing over whether a small shop farther down the street would still be open. The folded paper was a rough map, its creases worn white at the corners. One of them traced a route with his finger while the other insisted that the street they wanted was the next one over.

I watched them for a moment.

They had noticed me, made a mistake, corrected it, and moved on. Whatever they thought of my appearance, it had not consumed their evening. They had somewhere to go and a person to meet. I was an interruption, not the reason they had come.

That should have made the situation easier.

Instead, it left me unsure what to do with the fact that they had spoken to me at all.

The first boy looked back in my direction.

"Hey, do you live around here?"

There was the opening. Another question. Another chance to get ahead of the moment.

My mind immediately reached for the answer I had prepared earlier. If he was asking because I looked foreign, I could say that I was a student. If he wanted to know why I was in Kuoh, I could mention the academy. If he asked about school, I could use the same phrase from the dossier and keep the conversation short.

I had a response for a question he had not asked.

"No," I said. "I'm here for the academy."

He frowned slightly. "The academy?"

"Kuoh Academy."

"Oh." He glanced at his friend. "You mean you're taking the entrance exam?"

I nodded.

"That was today, wasn't it?"

"Yes."

He seemed to consider that for a second, then asked, "So where do you live?"

I opened my mouth.

Nothing useful came out.

The answer was simple. I lived in an apartment. It was somewhere in the area. I could have told him the name of the street, if I had been certain he would recognize it. But he was not asking about my school history or my nationality. He wanted to know whether I lived nearby, and I had answered a different question because I had expected one.

"Not far," I said.

It was the sort of answer that invited another question.

He looked at me for a moment, then nodded as though he had decided that was all he needed to know.

"Okay."

His friend folded the paper again.

"We should go. If we miss him, he'll complain all night."

"He complains all night anyway."

"Yeah, but then we have to listen to it."

They laughed, said goodbye to the woman behind the counter, and left. The bell sounded as the door opened, and the street noise rushed into the café before the door closed behind them.

I sat still with my hands around the cup.

That had gone badly.

Not disastrously. Nobody had threatened me, and nobody had even been particularly rude. I had simply managed to turn a normal question into an awkward exchange by preparing for the wrong conversation. The first prediction had worked because I had noticed what the woman was likely to ask. The second had failed because I had assumed that a new person would follow the same pattern.

I had wanted to be ready.

I had not been ready for the question that actually mattered.

The worst part was that, somewhere beneath the embarrassment, I had enjoyed the exchange. For a few seconds, I had been part of an ordinary conversation with people my age. They had made a joke, asked a question, and moved on. I had not known what to say, but I had been included long enough to feel the absence when they left.

I took another sip of my drink.

It had cooled.

"Great," I muttered.

The man with the newspaper looked up.

I lowered my cup.

He watched me for a moment, then returned to the same line of print. I could not tell whether he had heard me or had simply noticed the movement. Either way, there was no reason to make something of it.

I finished my food, drank the last of my drink, and carried the cup and plate to the counter. The woman accepted them with a nod and returned to her work.

"Thank you," I said.

"You're welcome. Have a good evening."

"You too."

I made it to the door before remembering that I had not asked how much I owed.

The woman called the amount after me.

I turned back, paid, and thanked her again.

She gave me a look that suggested she had already decided I was an odd child, but not one worth worrying about.

Outside, the air felt cooler against my face. The door closed behind me, cutting off the café's low murmur and leaving the street sounds more distinct. A vehicle passed at the far end of the road. Somewhere nearby, metal rattled as another shopkeeper lowered a shutter. The pavement sloped slightly toward the gutter, where a narrow line of water reflected the shop lights.

I stood beside the café window for a moment, watching people move past without looking in.

My first prediction had been right. My second had been wrong. That was the simplest way to put it, and the distinction made the evening feel more manageable than it had a few minutes earlier.

But the first prediction had not made me smooth or confident. It had merely helped me guess what someone might ask. I had still managed to embarrass myself with my own mouth.

And the second time, I had not even guessed the question correctly.

I started walking.

The commercial street continued beyond the café, its storefronts separated by narrow gaps and occasional side lanes. I passed a shop where someone was stacking boxes near the entrance, then a closed storefront with a faded sign above the shutter. A woman carrying groceries stepped around a bicycle parked too far into the pavement. She did not look at me. I moved aside to let her pass, then continued toward the next corner.

I tried to replay the conversation in my head.

The boy had asked whether I lived nearby. That was all. He might have been curious because I looked unfamiliar. He might have been making conversation. He might have wanted to know whether I could help him find the street on his map. I had no way to know which possibility had been closest to the truth, and I had made the question harder by answering something else.

Maybe I should have said that I had moved recently.

No. That would have invited questions about where I had moved from.

I could have said I was new to the area.

That was true enough, but then he might have asked where I had come from.

I could have told him that I was taking the entrance exam.

I had done that already.

I stopped at the corner and looked down the street.

The problem was not that I had failed to think of an answer. I had thought of too many answers, all for questions that had not been asked. The moment I needed to respond, I had tried to force the conversation into a shape I understood. When it refused to fit, I had nothing left.

I hated that.

I hated not knowing what people wanted from me. I hated that I could spend several minutes preparing for a conversation and still get caught by a simple question. I hated that a part of me had been pleased when the first prediction worked, as if I had discovered something important, only to have the next interaction expose how little I actually knew.

I rubbed the back of my neck.

"Okay. So that didn't work."

A man walking in the opposite direction glanced at me. I immediately looked away, then felt annoyed with myself for reacting as if I had been caught doing something wrong.

He kept walking.

The street did not care that I was trying to become a better person. It had shops to close, people to carry home, bicycles to avoid, and traffic moving through the intersections. Nothing paused while I worked through my feelings. If I stood at the corner long enough, somebody would eventually ask me to move.

I continued down the street.

The café was still visible behind me when I reached the next intersection. I could see its window between the passing figures, the warm interior broken into pieces by reflections from the street. Then a vehicle moved through my line of sight, and the view disappeared.

I had wanted a little relief from the apartment. I had found it, technically. I had eaten something, spoken to a few people, and returned to the problem that had been waiting for me at home.

At least the problem had become more specific.

I could not explain the educational history in the dossier because I had not lived it. That was not something I could fix by inventing a clever answer to one question. If I wanted to stop being caught off guard, I needed to know what the documents actually said and who had issued them.

I slowed beside a shop window and took my wallet out, checking the cash inside. I had spent a little, but not enough to matter tonight. I put the wallet away and kept walking.

The idea was practical. That made it easier to accept.

I would go back to the apartment, read the paperwork properly, and copy down the contact information. Tomorrow, I could find a public library or a municipal information desk and check a telephone directory. If the organization existed, I could find out where its office was and work out how to get there. If the address led nowhere, I would at least know that much.

It was not a plan for solving my entire life.

It was a way to find out one thing.

That seemed like a reasonable place to start.

By the time I returned to the building, the sky had darkened enough that the windows across the lane reflected more of the street than the rooms behind them. I climbed the stairs, unlocked my door, and stepped back into the apartment.

The quiet was waiting where I had left it.

I put my wallet and phone on the table, hung my coat over the back of a chair, and went to the kitchen for water. When I returned, the envelope was still beside the place where I had been sitting. The placard had slipped partly out from under the flap.

I pushed it back inside.

Then I opened the envelope and spread the documents across the table.

The first pages were the ones I had already seen. Application forms, examination material, the official-looking education history. I read the entries more carefully this time, moving from one line to the next instead of scanning for a phrase that might reassure me. The paperwork had been assembled to answer the questions an institution was likely to ask. It did not tell me how to answer the questions a person might ask after reading it.

I found the education/contact line again.

The name was printed in formal type, followed by an address and a telephone number. I copied both onto a scrap of paper, checking each character against the original. The address included the name of an administrative annex in the Minami-Kuoh area, associated with the Kanagawa Prefectural Educational Development Association.

I stopped writing.

That was a real lead, at least in the limited sense that it was a name and an address I could try to verify. I did not know whether the association existed, whether the annex operated at that location, or whether anyone there would recognize the documents. I could not assume the paperwork was fraudulent just because I did not understand it. I could not assume it was legitimate just because it had carried me through registration.

I had been doing too much of both.

I turned the scrap over and wrote the name again, more carefully. Beneath it, I added a note to check a local telephone directory and a map before attempting the journey. I would need to know whether the address was reachable by train or bus, how much the trip might cost, and whether I could get there without wasting half a day going in the wrong direction.

My dead phone sat beside the papers.

I pressed the power button once. Nothing happened.

"Of course."

I set it aside and pulled a paper map from the drawer where I had been keeping miscellaneous household items. It was not the right scale to plan every turn, but it gave me a starting point. I found the general area, then marked the location as best I could from the information on the page. The pencil line was faint. I went over it once, then stopped before I made the mark so thick that it covered the place name.

The map did not tell me what I would find there.

It did not promise that the address was current or that the people responsible for my documents would help me. It could not tell me what to say if someone asked who I was, or why a sixteen-year-old without a normal school history was trying to verify an educational record.

I folded it along its old creases and placed it beside the copied address.

Tomorrow, I would start at the library. I would check the directory, verify the name, and find out whether the office was where the documents claimed it was. If I needed to travel farther, I would work out the route before leaving. No guessing the transfer. No assuming that the first answer would cover the next question.

I read the address one final time.

It was a small thing to do. A name, a number, a place on a map. It might lead to an explanation, or it might lead to another question. I had no way to know yet.

But it was something I could check.

I put the papers back into the envelope, leaving the copied address and map on the table. Then I placed the pencil across the map so I would see them when I woke up.

The apartment remained quiet. The evening had moved on without resolving anything, and the life I was supposed to inhabit still contained more gaps than answers.

I looked at the mark on the map.

Tomorrow, I would find out whether that address existed.

For tonight, that was enough.
