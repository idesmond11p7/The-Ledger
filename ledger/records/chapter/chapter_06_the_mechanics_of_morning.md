Chapter 6: The Mechanics of Morning
The futon didn’t feel like an alien landing site anymore. It just felt like a stiff mattress over tatami that needed airing out.

When I opened my eyes, the ceiling was the same pale plaster with the faint hairline fracture near the light fixture. The light filtering around the plastic edges of the roller blind was a thin, cold grey, tinted with the blue of an overcast six-thirty in early spring.

I lay there for a minute, letting my breathing settle into the room.

My shoulder blades pressed against the floor with less surface area than I was used to. That was still the first thing every morning—the sense of a revised perimeter. The frame under the blanket was narrow, light enough that shifting my hip bone against the cotton padding made a dull, precise point of pressure. My hair was caught under my left armpit. It was always caught somewhere now. When I rolled over, it dragged against the nape of my neck like a heavy, cool skein of silk, strands sticking to the corner of my mouth.

I blew them away, sat up, and ran both hands back through it.

Black hair, dense enough to hold water like a sponge, with that weird, stubborn dark-teal sheen whenever the light hit the grain sideways. It fell well past my collarbones now. In my old life, my hair had been thick, coarse, and trimmed every four weeks at a strip-mall barber who smelled of barbicide and menthol tobacco. Here, if I didn't gather it, it got into my eyes, my collar, the toast butter, everything.

I found a black elastic band on the low wooden table beside the lamp and tied it back behind my neck, pulling it tight until the skin around my temples lifted slightly.

The apartment was cold. The kind of concrete-box cold that seeped in through the balcony slider overnight. I got to my feet, my bare soles sticking faintly to the floorboards of the kitchen corridor, and turned the dial on the two-burner gas range. A blue circle of flame hissed into life with a heavy flump. I set a dented aluminum kettle over it, then walked into the narrow washroom to deal with my face.

The mirror over the plastic basin didn’t lie, but it still startled me if I looked too quickly.

Ira Lowe looked back.

Sixteen years old. Pale skin that seemed completely unacquainted with sunburn, resting over facial bones that were almost irritatingly tidy—a small, straight nose, jawline tapering into a rounded chin, lips that naturally sat in a faint, neutral line. And the eyes: vivid, high-saturation emerald green, rimmed by thick, dark lashes that looked like they belonged on a high-end department store cosmetics advert.

"Morning, stranger," I muttered.

The voice was soft. Not a squeak, but light, pitched high in the chest cavity, clear as a bell. It didn't sound like a boy who had spent seventeen years yelling across basketball courts and arguing over rusted mufflers. It sounded like a choir student who had never missed a Sunday service.

I grabbed the plastic toothbrush, loaded it with chalky mint paste, and scrubbed until my gums stung.

Today was the exam.

Yesterday had been the desperate reconnaissance mission—the panic of waking up inside someone else's skin, finding a foreign alien life stuffed into a shoebox apartment, fumbling through railway lines until I found the name Kuoh, and staring up at the stone gates of an academy that looked like it had been lifted from an architectural digest of Prussian palaces.

Today wasn't reconnaissance. Today was the actual door.

In the kitchen, the kettle whistled with a thin, wheezing shriek. I killed the burner, poured hot water into a chipped mug over a packet of instant drip coffee, and watched the dark foam bloom over the paper filter. The smell—roasted, bitter, slightly burnt chicory—helped more than anything else to anchor my skull to the room.

On the low table lay the brown envelope.

I sat down cross-legged, my knees clicking faintly, and checked the contents for the fourth time.

The application slip for Kuoh Academy. The official stamp in vermilion ink was square and crisp over the lower right corner. My passport-style photo was stapled to the top left: Ira looking dead at the lens with an expression of polite, vacant melancholy. My birth date. My registered address.

Next to the papers lay three pre-sharpened HB pencils, a soft plastic eraser still in its cardboard sleeve, a pencil sharpener shaped like a tiny metal wedge, and a worn black leather wallet containing eleven thousand yen in crisp, folded bills and a small handful of heavy silver hundred-yen coins.

Beside that was my phone. A silver Panasonic flip handset, scratched along the hinge, dead silent. In 2005, a phone was a tool for short text messages and quick calls, not an endless window into the collective panic of the planet. It sat on the table like a folded cigarette case. I slipped it into my coat pocket along with the wallet.

I dressed carefully.

There was no official uniform for applicants from independent or overseas schooling backgrounds, so I had to make do with what Ira’s tiny closet provided: a charcoal-grey wool duffel coat that hung slightly long past my knees, black tailored trousers that were an inch too wide in the waist—cinched tight with a stiff leather belt—and a plain white collared shirt under a navy-blue crewneck sweater.

The clothes smelled faintly of cedar and dry cleaning. When I pulled the coat on, the heavy wool settled against my shoulders with a reassuring weight. It hid the narrowness of my chest and the slender taper of my ribs. In a loose coat, at least, I looked less like an illustration and more like a person walking down a street to take a test.

I slid the envelope and stationery into a plain canvas shoulder bag, jammed my feet into a pair of scuffed black leather lace-ups at the genkan, and tied the double knots tight.

I took one breath of the apartment’s stale, heated air, turned the latch, and stepped out.

The outside was loud, bright, and smelled of wet bitumen.

The morning chill hit my face with the force of a cold wet towel. The sky above the apartment block was a vast, seamless apron of pale slate, stitched across by thousands of black utility cables that looped from concrete pole to concrete pole like drunken handwriting.

A small delivery van—a white Kei truck with a dented rear fender—rattled past down the two-lane street, its engine whining like an overgrown sewing machine. On the opposite corner, a woman in a quilted apron was rolling up the corrugated steel shutter of a dry cleaner's, the metal slats screaming against their rusted runners with a sharp clack-clack-clack that echoed between the three-story apartment fronts.

I turned left toward the station.

My feet found the pavement with a different stride than they used to. Ira’s legs were shorter than Jonah’s by maybe two inches, but his center of gravity was lower, cleaner. I didn't clomp. The leather soles made a crisp, polite tapping sound on the tarmac as I navigated the narrow strip between the roadside drainage ditch and the line of parked utility bicycles.

A middle-aged salaryman in a beige trench coat hurried past me, smelling of stale tobacco and hair tonic, his black briefcase swinging close to his knee. As he cut in front to avoid a puddle, his elbow brushed my sleeve.

"Ah, sumimasen," he muttered reflexively, barely looking down, dipping his head before marching on toward the station entrance.

The voice had carried that particular, automatic deference usually reserved for teenage girls or junior office clerks. I looked down at my coat, felt the strand of hair that had slipped free of the band tickling the edge of my jaw, and shoved my hands deep into my pockets.

Right. Don't worry about it. Just keep moving.

The station entrance was a gaping concrete mouth beneath the elevated tracks, swallowing an unbroken stream of commuters. The air inside smelled of ozone, damp wool, paper newsprint, and the sweet, fried-sugar reek of the waffle stand tucked beside the stairs.

I didn't have an automated Suica card yet—those were just starting to dominate the turnstiles—so I joined the short queue at the ticket vending machines. The mechanical clatter was relentless: buttons thunking inward, coins dropping into metal hoppers with a dull chink-chink, the sudden hiss of automated ticket gates snapping shut when someone mistimed their approach.

I looked at the fare map above the machine. It was a massive, spider-webbed acrylic diagram of coloured lines and black kanji.

Kuoh. Transfer at Minami-Chofu, take the private line outward.

I fed a thousand-yen note into the slot. The machine swallowed it with a hungry mechanical hum, clicked three times, and spat out a small, stiff paper ticket with a magnetic brown back, along with my change in heavy coins.

I took the ticket, walked to the turnstile, and slid it into the narrow slot. It vanished, the gate flashed a quiet green bulb, and the ticket popped out of the slot three feet ahead with a tiny snick. I snatched it mid-stride and joined the cattle drive up the concrete stairs toward Platform 3.

The wind on the elevated platform was biting. It smelled of steel brake dust and distant rain.

A train was already pulling in. The yellow-striped nose of the JR commuter train slid along the edge of the platform with a heavy, grinding squeal of iron on iron, the wind of its arrival flattening my coat against my shins. The double doors hissed open in unison, and the platform breathed out a wave of warm, humid, human air.

I squeezed into the middle carriage, finding a pocket of space between a vertical handrail and the back of a plastic seat bench.

The train lurched.

It wasn't a sudden jolt, but a massive, hydraulic displacement of mass that shoved everyone three inches to the left. The steel wheels under the floorboards began their rhythmic, hammering chant—ka-tunk, ka-tunk, ka-tunk—accelerating as the grey concrete of the platform gave way to a blur of brick walls, rooftop water tanks, and laundry flapping on narrow apartment balconies.

Sunlight broke through the cloud cover suddenly, cutting diagonally through the smeared glass of the train windows.

It was blinding. The light caught every micro-scratch in the acrylic glass, transforming the window into a fractured sheet of pale gold. It struck the commuters opposite me: the sharp, white crease of a starched collar; the fine, dusty texture of an older woman's wool scarf; the blue-black shine of school uniforms.

Directly across from me, holding the leather strap above her head, stood a woman who looked to be in her mid-twenties.

She wasn't an anime character. She didn't have lavender hair or an impossible waist. But she was undeniably, startlingly striking in the way only real, well-put-together people are on an otherwise miserable morning commute.

She wore a sharply tailored camel-wool coat over an ivory silk blouse, the collar open just enough to show the hollow of her throat and a thin silver chain. Her skirt was dark, cut just above the knee, revealing legs in sheer, dark-tinted stockings that ended in glossy black leather heels planted firmly against the ribbed rubber flooring of the train. Her hair was rich, chestnut-brown, gathered into an effortless loose twist at the back of her head, with soft, curled tendrils framing high cheekbones and a mouth painted in a quiet, berry-red gloss.

She was holding a paperback novel with one hand, her long, manicured fingers turning the thin page without looking down.

As the train took a long, banked curve over a river bridge, the whole carriage leaned. Her shoulder brushed mine gently—just a soft, warm bump of wool against wool.

A scent came with it. Not heavy perfume, but something crisp and floral—expensive citrus, clean soap, and the faint, bitter edge of black tea.

I caught myself looking. Not even subtly. My brain, which had spent seventeen years as an awkward teenage boy, was currently firing off all the standard, embarrassing alerts. She had that effortless, adult composure that made sixteen-year-old boys feel like they were made of wooden sticks and grease.

Then she glanced down from her book.

Her eyes were hazel, sharp, and slightly tired around the corners. She caught me staring.

I froze, my jaw locking, my internal Jonah desperately preparing for the cold, disgusted glare every teenage boy deserves when he's caught ogling a commuter twice his social bracket.

Instead, her expression softened.

She looked at my face—really looked at it—and her lips curved into a tiny, amused, almost maternal smile.

"Are you all right?" she asked. Her voice was low, smooth, cutting easily through the rumble of the train. "You look pale. Is it an exam day?"

She was looking at my canvas bag, the pencil case visible through the unzipped top edge.

"Uh," I said.

My voice came out light, slightly breathless, and high.

"Yeah," I managed, clearing my throat and trying to drop the pitch half an octave. It didn't drop much. "Entrance test."

"I thought so," she said, her smile widening just enough to crinkle the skin near her eyes. She shifted her grip on the overhead strap, giving me another three inches of breathing room against the wall. "Don't be nervous. With a face like that, the interviewers will probably pass you before you even sit down. Do your best, okay?"

She gave me a gentle, conspiratorial nod, then looked back down at her paperback, turning the page with a clean flick of her thumb.

I stared at the floorboards between my boots.

My face was burning. It was burning hot enough to fry an egg on my forehead.

She hadn't thought I was a creep. She had looked at me, seen a pretty, fragile, nervous girl in an oversized coat heading off to a high school entrance exam, and decided to offer some big-sister encouragement.

Jesus Christ.

I pinched the bridge of my nose, feeling the delicate, narrow bone beneath the skin. This is my life now. I am officially non-threatening. I am an ornament.

The train began to brake, the air compressors under the chassis sighing loudly as the automated voice chimed: “Next stop, Minami-Chofu. Minami-Chofu. Doors will open on the left side.”

The woman closed her book with a quiet thwip, slipped it into her leather handbag, and stepped toward the opening doors without looking back. I let the crowd carry me out onto the platform behind her, the crisp morning wind immediately clearing the citrus scent from my head.

The transfer concourse was an underground concrete cavern, smelling of damp tile and grease.

I had forty minutes before the registration window opened at Kuoh, and my stomach was suddenly, aggressively hollow. That cup of instant coffee had burned away into pure stomach acid.

Beside the stairs to the private railway platforms was a small, glowing kiosk—a kiosk with a red canvas awning and an aluminum warming cabinet displaying rows of hot canned beverages and paper-wrapped buns.

"One pork bun, please," I said, stepping up to the waist-high counter.

The old man behind the glass had a white paper cap and hands like wrinkled butcher paper. He didn't look at me for more than a second. "One-twenty."

I handed him two hundred-yen coins. He used metal tongs to lift a steaming, paper-bottomed nikuman from the wire rack inside the steamer, dropped it into a thin plastic wrapper, and pushed it across the stainless-steel counter with my eighty yen in change.

"Watch out, it's hot," he grunted.

It was. The bun was scorching against the palm of my hand, the thick white dough soft, yielding, and smelling intensely of steamed yeast, ginger, and braised pork.

I stepped into a corner out of the pedestrian flow, leaning against a square concrete pillar covered in faded posters for 2005 cinema releases—some local drama, an animated film, a domestic thriller—and peeled back the paper base.

The first bite tore open the steam pocket. Hot, salty broth hit my tongue, followed by the rich, fatty chew of minced pork, green onions, and sweet soy.

I chewed slowly, watching the boots and shoes churn past.

It was good. It was stupidly, miraculously good. In my old life, breakfast had been dry cereal eaten standing over the sink while watching the kitchen clock tick toward seven-fifteen. Here, standing under an underground transit ceiling in a town whose name I still had to double-check on my ticket, the pork fat and steam felt like the only completely solid things in the universe.

I swallowed, took a second bite, and let the warmth settle into the pit of my ribs.

I wasn't a ghost anymore. Ghosts don't have to digest pork.

The private line out to Kuoh was different.

The train was shorter—only four cars—and the commuters were no longer the grey, hurried salarymen of central Tokyo. There were more students here, kids in dark navy blazers with polished brass buttons, older women carrying woven shopping baskets, and local tradesmen with canvas tool wraps tucked under their arms.

I almost blew the transfer.

At the platform divide, the overhead signage split into two arrows: one for the Local, one for the Rapid. In my head, Rapid meant faster, which meant better. I had one foot over the threshold of the Rapid car when I glanced at the small, paper timetable posted on the carriage door.

Kuoh: Passed.

The Rapid bypassed it entirely. Kuoh wasn't a major hub; it was an affluent suburb, a dead-end pocket of green that the express trains ignored on their way to the industrial coast.

I yanked my foot back just as the warning chime sounded, the rubber edge of the closing door catching the tail of my coat with a faint thump.

A group of junior high boys on the platform snickered behind their hands.

"Stupid," I muttered to myself, stepping back toward the wooden benches. My heart was thumping in my throat. Not panic—just the cold, sharp adrenaline of a near-miss.

I didn't freeze, though. I didn't sit down and wait for someone to save me. I walked over to the pillar, studied the blue Local timetable, confirmed the arrival time of the next train—seven minutes—and stood right behind the yellow line with my hands in my pockets, breathing evenly through my nose.

You fix the problem. You don't whine about the track.

When the Local train arrived, it was half-empty.

The seats were covered in faded green velour that puffed a faint cloud of dust when I sat down. As the train cleared the concrete trench of the station, the city began to unravel.

The transition wasn't cinematic; it was gradual, mundane, and vast. The concrete office towers shrank into five-story brick apartment buildings, which gave way to narrow streets of two-story detached homes with tiled roofs and tiny, immaculate carports holding boxy silver hatchbacks.

Then came the greenery.

It wasn't wild forest. It was managed, wealthy Japanese suburbia: massive, dark-green zelkova trees leaning over high concrete walls; bamboo groves tucked between modern residential developments; wide, clean asphalt avenues lined with ginkgo trees whose bare branches reached up toward the clearing sky like grey bone.

The light changed. Out here, away from the glass canyons of the city center, the morning sky felt huge. The grey cloud deck was breaking apart into long, torn ribbons of white, letting down broad, brilliant pillars of pale gold sunlight that washed over the rooftops.

The automated speaker chimed with a clear, two-tone bell:

“Kuoh. Kuoh. Please check that you have not left any belongings behind.”

Kuoh Station didn't look like an annex of Tokyo. It looked like an old European railway depot that had been meticulously rebuilt by Japanese civil engineers.

The roof was a dark vaulted iron framework set with frosted glass panes, letting the morning light fall across clean, brick-paved platforms. There was no trash. There were no cigarette butts crushed into the expansion joints. Even the air smelled different—cooler, carrying a sharp, clean scent of cedarwood, damp moss, and mountain earth that seemed to drift down from the low, forested ridges that hemmed the town in from the north.

I walked out through the wicket, slipped my used ticket into the collection box, and stood on the station forecourt.

The town of Kuoh sloped upward.

Wide streets paved in smooth, dark tarmac wound gently up the hill, flanked by broad stone sidewalks, wrought-iron streetlamps that looked like they belonged in nineteenth-century Vienna, and high, whitewashed perimeter walls that concealed sprawling residential estates.

And at the top of that gentle rise, dominating the entire district like an ancient fortress converted into a sanctuary, was Kuoh Academy.

From this distance, the scale was clearer than it had been yesterday. The main building was a massive, three-story neo-classical structure of pale, cream-coloured stone, capped with high mansard roofs of weathered slate. Flanking it were modern extensions—glass-fronted wings and broad athletic pavilions—all connected by covered stone colonnades that cut through immaculately kept lawns.

It was intimidating as hell.

It wasn't a high school. It was an institution. It had the heavy, silent gravity of money so old that it didn't need to brag about itself.

I adjusted the strap of my canvas bag, pulled my collar against the wind, and began the walk up the hill.

The approach road was already busy.

Groups of applicants were moving in the same direction, easy to spot by their nervous posture and their plain, dark junior-high blazers. Some were accompanied by mothers in modest wool coats and pearls; others walked alone, clutching their plastic document folders like shields.

Then there were the actual Kuoh students.

The academy had only recently transitioned from an elite, ultra-traditional all-girls private school into a co-educational institution. That much I had gleaned from the paperwork yesterday. But seeing it in the flesh was another matter.

The current female students—the second- and third-years—wore uniforms that were frankly absurd in their attention to detail: high-waisted black skirts with white frilled trim, white shirts under tailored black vests with crimson ribbon ties, and long black capes that fluttered around their calves as they walked. They moved in tight, quiet clusters, their voices low and musical, ignoring the applicants with the casual, devastating indifference of people who already owned the ground they walked on.

I passed beneath the massive wrought-iron front gates.

The ironwork was black, spiked, and bore a complex crest in the center—two intertwined wings resting over an open book and a stylized sword. The gravel on the main approach crunch-crunched under hundreds of leather soles, a rhythmic, oceanic sound that filled the broad avenue between the double rows of ancient cherry trees.

The buds on the branches were still tight, dark nodules, not yet ready to burst. It was too early for blossoms.

"Applicants for General Studies, please proceed to the West Hall entrance!"

The voice came through a handheld megaphone, flat, slightly distorted, but completely authoritative.

Standing on the low stone steps of the administrative wing was a folding table covered in green baize. Behind it stood three teachers in dark suits, alongside several older students wearing white armbands marked with the school crest.

I joined the line.

The boy in front of me was trembling slightly. I could see the fine, rapid vibration of his coat hem. He was gripping an envelope so hard that his knuckles were white, his eyes fixed on the stone pavers between his boots.

I looked past him, watching the machinery of the place work.

The registration was fast, efficient, and cold. You stepped up, presented your examination slip, an administrator checked your name against a thick, leather-bound register, an older student handed you a printed plastic desk card with your exam number, and you were directed into the holding area.

"Next," one of the teachers called out.

The trembling boy stepped forward, bowed too low, fumbled his paperwork onto the baize, and stammered his name. The teacher, an older man with wire-rimmed glasses and a thin grey mustache, didn't even look up as he inked a red seal onto the boy's form.

"Second floor, Lecture Room 2B. Move along."

The boy scrambled off like he had been granted a reprieve from an execution.

I stepped up to the green baize.

The wind caught my duffel coat, pulling the front open slightly. I reached into my canvas bag, drew out the brown envelope, and laid the stamped form flat on the cloth.

"Ira Lowe," I said.

My voice was clear. It didn't waver.

The teacher paused. His pen hovered an inch above the paper. He looked up, his small, dark eyes resting first on the name printed in roman letters, then drifting up to my face.

He didn't say anything for two seconds. He just looked at the pale skin, the emerald eyes, the long black hair tied back against the collar of the duffel coat. His eyebrows twitched together in a tiny, almost imperceptible knot of confusion before his gaze dropped back to the gender box on the form.

Male.

He cleared his throat, a dry, abrasive rattle.

"Overseas transfer track?" he asked, his tone shifting from bored routine to something faintly cautious.

"Independent curriculum," I replied evenly. "Everything is in the dossier."

He dipped his seal into the vermilion paste, pressed it firmly against the corner of the slip with a sharp, heavy thunk, and slid a blue plastic placard across the baize toward me.

Applicant No. 1044.

"Third floor, North Hall, Room 301," he said. He didn't smile, but his eyes lingered on me as I picked up the placard. "Pencils and erasers only on the desks. Bags in the rear cubbies. The examination begins at nine sharp."

"Understood. Thank you."

I took the card, gave a shallow, polite bow, and turned toward the wide stone archway of the main hall.

The interior of Kuoh Academy was dead quiet compared to the courtyard.

The floors were polished parquetry—dark, honey-coloured oak laid in a herringbone pattern that gleamed under the high, arched windows. The walls were panelled in dark mahogany up to waist height, topped with white plaster that smelled faintly of old floor wax, chalk, and lime.

As I walked down the central corridor toward the grand staircase, the crowd of applicants began to thin, channeled off into different wings by student guides.

That was where the foot traffic shifted.

It wasn't noisy; it was an absence. A dozen paces ahead of me, the center line of the corridor simply emptied out. People didn't scramble, but they moved aside early, stepping toward the wainscoting before she even reached the junction. Two senior boys who had been speaking in low, rushed murmurs leaned back against the wall, their chatter cutting off mid-sentence, their hands falling straight to their seams. A faculty member—a man with greying hair in a dark, tailored three-piece suit—stood half a pace behind her, waiting with a dark ledger pressed against his chest.

She was standing near the foot of the west gallery archway.

She was tall—several inches taller than the body I was wearing—with a slender, clean verticality to her frame that made her posture seem architectural rather than rigid. Her uniform was the academy's black and crimson, but it hung without a fold or wrinkle out of place. Her hair was cut into a jaw-length bob of midnight black, sheared in a line so straight against the pale skin of her neck that it looked measured with a rule.

She wore square-framed spectacles with thin dark wire rims resting on a narrow, delicate nose. Her features had an unusual, severe symmetry—the transition from cheekbone to chin drawn so cleanly that my eye hesitated trying to process it. She didn't look sick or cold, despite the morning frost outside; her skin had the smooth, untouched finish of fine marble.

The faculty member offered a slim leather folder, his voice kept low. "The third-tier annex registers have been finalized, but if the arrival numbers from the eastern rail link require an extension—"

"No," she said.

Her voice was quiet, dry, and unhurried. It carried cleanly through the hall without being raised.

"The railway schedule was published six weeks in advance," she said. "Those who fail to anticipate municipal maintenance lack the fundamental discipline Kuoh requires. Close the registry at eight-forty-five."

"Understood," the man murmured. He lowered his gaze and stepped back half a pace without an ounce of friction.

She closed the folder with a light, deliberate movement of her fingers.

As she turned toward the corridor, the high clerestory windows caught her profile.

And right then, my eyes caught something wrong on the floor.

The morning sun was cutting diagonally through the glass, throwing long slashes of light across the honey-coloured parquetry. As she shifted her footing, her shadow fell across the oak planks toward the wall.

It was too long.

For half a heartbeat, the dark shape cast by her hem and shoulders reached five or six inches past where the geometry of the window mullions should have allowed—a sharp, heavy blade of grey that seemed to cling to the timber with an unnatural density.

I blinked.

When my eyes focused again, the floor was ordinary. Just a pale grey shadow resting over the wood grain, angled exactly with the light. A trick of the double-paned leaded glass and the morning glare.

Then her chin lifted, and her gaze swept the hall.

Behind her glasses, her eyes were a dark, deep violet.

They moved across the students until they reached me.

At close range, her eye line sat clearly above mine, but she didn't tilt her head. Her gaze simply lowered by a fraction of an inch. It paused on the blue placard gripped in my hand, moved to my duffel coat, and then to my face.

Her expression didn't change. The line of her mouth remained completely still.

Then her eyes moved on, continuing down the length of the corridor as if she had simply registered another piece of furniture.

She turned toward the western administrative wing. Her shoes made almost no sound against the parquet. The students against the wall didn't resume walking until the hem of her dark cape cleared the archway, and only then did the quiet shuffle and murmur of the hallway return.

I stood there for three seconds, the cold plastic edge of Applicant No. 1044 pressing into the meat of my palm.

Who was that?

The question had no answer, and I didn't have time to look for one. The older teacher had bowed to her like she owned the building, and my eyes were apparently playing tricks on me with the morning light.

I shook my head once, clearing the visual residue, and exhaled through my teeth.

Not your concern. Pass the exam first.

I found the central staircase—a massive, sweeping flight of marble steps with a polished brass banister—and began the climb to the third floor.

Room 301 was a high-ceilinged lecture hall with five tiers of long wooden desks, each equipped with an inkwell slot and a brass numbering plate.

The room smelled of dry radiator heat and cold slate. Sunlight was pouring through four massive, ten-foot-tall sash windows along the north wall, illuminating the millions of dust motes dancing in the quiet air.

There were about thirty applicants already seated. Nobody was talking. The silence was heavy, thick, and suffocating, punctuated only by the dry, occasional cough of a nervous kid or the faint, nervous rattle of a wooden pencil against a desktop.

I walked down the stepped aisle to the middle tier, found desk forty-four, and slid into the bench.

The wood of the desk was old, scarred by decades of ballpoint pens and compass points, polished down by hundreds of forearms until it felt as smooth as river stone. I took my three pencils from the bag, lined them up parallel to the brass number plate, placed the eraser beside them, and set the blue placard upright in the slot provided.

Then I took off my duffel coat, folded it over the back of the wooden bench, and sat down.

My canvas bag went into the wooden cubby under the seat.

I was alone with the pencils.

I leaned back against the uprights of the bench, folded my hands in my lap, and looked out the third-floor window.

From up here, the view was vast.

You could see over the slate roofs of the academy's outbuildings, past the high stone perimeter wall, and down into the sprawling green basin of Kuoh. The town looked peaceful, orderly, and entirely unaware of whatever machinery kept it running. Beyond the houses, the dark, pine-covered hills rolled upward into the pale blue morning, their ridges sharp against the horizon.

My chest felt strange.

Not tight. Not panicked.

Just... still.

Yesterday, I had been a dead boy who woke up in a terrifying, foreign cage, running on pure, frantic instinct, desperately looking for an address that made sense. I had been terrified that the world would realize I was an imposter, that some invisible referee would blow a whistle, grab me by the scruff of my neck, and drag me back into the dark where Jonah Laurence belonged.

That hadn't happened.

The train had taken my money. The woman on the commute had given me a gentle smile. The old man at the kiosk had handed me a pork bun. The administrator at the gate had stamped my paper.

The world didn't care who I had been. The world was too busy moving forward. It just saw a boy named Ira Lowe who had managed to find a coat, buy a ticket, and climb the hill to take a test.

I looked down at my hands.

The fingers were long, pale, and narrow. The knuckles were clean, completely unscarred. They didn't look like my hands. They never would.

Fine, I thought. Then they're mine now.

At the front of the lecture hall, the heavy oak door swung open with a dry, mechanical creak.

A proctor in a dark charcoal suit walked in, carrying a thick stack of sealed brown paper folios bound with red string. Behind him, another teacher carried an armful of wooden clipboards.

The proctor didn't look at us. He walked to the wide mahogany lectern, set the folios down with a heavy, final thwack, and drew a silver pocket watch from his waistcoat.

He clicked the watch open, checked the face, and looked up at the wall clock above the blackboard.

The long black second hand was sweeping upward, ticking through the final quadrant of the dial.

Eight-fifty-nine.

Every kid in the room seemed to stop breathing at the same time. The silence in the hall became absolute—so dense that I could hear the faint, high-frequency hiss of the steam radiator under the window and the sound of my own pulse tapping against my temples.

I reached out, picked up the first HB pencil, and felt the sharp, six-sided cedar casing settle against the middle finger of my right hand.

Above our heads, high in the central tower of the academy, the great iron bell began to swing.

The sound arrived first as a low, physical vibration through the floorboards—a deep, resonant hum that rattled the loose glass in the tall sash windows—before the actual chime struck.

GONG.

A massive, bronze, two-tone strike that rolled out across the slate roofs, echoing over the courtyard and out into the valley below.

The proctor snapped his pocket watch shut with a sharp click.

"It is nine o'clock," he said.

His voice was entirely devoid of drama. He reached for the red string on the first folio, snapped it with a single, practiced tug, and began to separate the papers.

"The examination will now begin."
