Chapter 9

My shoulder satchel kept slipping from my shoulder.

I shifted the strap back into place and pulled the apartment door shut behind me, checking the lock once before stepping away. The parcel inside the bag knocked lightly against my hip. Kenji had wrapped it in enough brown paper and tape to survive a minor flood, which seemed excessive for two replacement clamps.

The morning was cold. Not unpleasantly so, but cold enough that I regretted leaving my gloves on the kitchen table.

In the satchel I had the parcel, my commuter pass, my phone, and a folded sheet of grid paper with Kenji's directions. I had checked the directions three times already.

That was probably two more times than necessary.

Kenji had caught me by the shoe lockers yesterday, just as I was trying to untangle my umbrella from the rack.

"You're heading past the Kita-Kuoh district tomorrow anyway, right?"

He had been shifting a gym duffel between his shoulders. His uniform collar was crooked, and there was a faint smell of fried noodles clinging to his jacket.

"My uncle runs a framing shop behind the old timber yard on the canal. He ordered two replacement clamps for the mitre saw, but he's injured his hip and I have club practice until five."

He had pushed a flat cardboard carton into my hands before I could answer.

"It's just the clamps. Drop them on the counter, tell him I'll come by tomorrow to sweep up, and you're done."

"I don't know the north loop."

"You can't miss it. Take the bus until the blue municipal signs, walk along the canal road past the batching plant. Green metal roof. Big cedar sign."

He had then added several more directions, apparently remembering them in the wrong order.

I had written them down.

The parcel was light enough. The obligation was simple enough. There wasn't much to think about.

That was why I had agreed.

The bus was already crowded when I boarded near the central station. I found a seat beside the rear exit and settled the satchel across my knees.

Kuoh moved past the scratched window in familiar layers.

The central district was awake but not busy yet. Grocers were dragging plastic crates onto the pavement. A fishmonger hosed yesterday's scales toward a drain while a delivery scooter waited with its engine running. The bakery near the crossing had its shutters halfway raised, and a line of schoolchildren had formed outside a convenience store, arguing over something printed on the back of a snack packet.

The bus passed through the commercial district and began climbing.

The buildings gradually thinned. Narrow shopfronts gave way to walled residential compounds, grey-tiled roofs and clipped pine branches extending above moss-darkened concrete. Utility cables sagged between poles, crossing the pale sky in irregular black lines.

Beyond the roofs, the academy ridge rose above the neighbourhood.

The western buildings were mostly concealed by trees. From this angle, only a copper-green spire showed above the dark slope, catching the morning light whenever the bus passed between buildings.

I watched it until the road curved away.

The northern basin looked different.

The residential walls gave way to workshops, storage yards and low industrial buildings. Some had been painted recently. Others had corrugated shutters with rust spreading around the bolts. Gravel trucks stood in open concrete yards, their engines idling while workers in heavy jackets moved between stacks of timber and bundled metal.

The canal ran beside the road.

It was narrower than the southern stretch, with battered sheet-piling along its banks and patches of pale foam gathering near the sluice gates. The water moved slowly beneath the morning light.

I got off beside a rusted bus shelter.

"Kita-Kuoh, Fourth Sluice," the recorded announcement said as the doors closed behind me.

The air smelled of diesel, wet sand and freshly cut cedar.

I checked Kenji's directions.

Two hundred yards north. Past the telephone poles waiting for removal. Green roof.

The shop was difficult to mistake once I reached it. A weathered wooden shed stood behind the timber yard, its green metal roof streaked with rust. A cedar sign above the entrance read MIZUNO TIMBER.

A smaller sign beside the wicket instructed delivery drivers to use the office window.

I pushed the wicket open.

An older man sat behind the counter with a mug of tea and a ledger. He wore a blue quilted vest over a grey sweater, and his reading glasses had slipped low on his nose.

He looked at the parcel.

"Kenji?"

"Yes, sir. He asked me to drop these off."

"Boy couldn't bring them himself?"

"He has club practice."

The man made a noise that suggested this explanation had not improved his opinion of the situation.

He took the parcel, examined the tape and turned it over.

"Clamps?"

"That's what he told me."

"Then he remembered to send the right box for once."

He placed it beneath the counter and returned to his ledger.

I waited a moment.

"Was there anything else?"

"No. That's all."

The man looked up again, apparently noticing that I was still standing there.

"You're going back toward the station?"

"Eventually."

He reached beneath the counter and slid a warm can of sweetened coffee across the wood.

"Take it. Wind comes off the ridge."

"Thank you."

I tucked it into my jacket pocket.

The handover had taken less than three minutes.

I stepped outside and checked the time.

There was no reason to return immediately. The apartment would be empty, the morning still had several hours left in it, and I had spent enough of the previous week sitting indoors waiting for things to happen.

I had no particular destination.

That was the advantage of not having one.

I walked south along the canal road.

The first stretch was industrial. A concrete batching plant stood behind a high fence, its mixing machinery turning with a slow mechanical groan. A worker in a reflective vest was washing dust from the loading apron. Beyond it, a row of small garages opened onto the road, each with a different collection of tools, tyres and half-disassembled machinery arranged around its entrance.

The canal narrowed beneath a road bridge.

I crossed it, then followed the pavement toward a small cluster of shops built around an old intersection. A vegetable seller was arranging daikon and winter cabbage beneath a faded awning. Two men in work jackets stood outside a noodle shop, arguing about a delivery that had apparently arrived in the wrong quantity.

The smell of broth drifted across the pavement.

I considered stopping.

Then I remembered I had already eaten breakfast.

I kept walking.

The district gradually became less industrial. The larger workshops gave way to repair shops and family businesses, then to narrow residential streets with low walls and bicycles parked beneath plastic covers.

A public noticeboard stood beside a small community hall. Several notices had been pinned over older ones, their corners curling in the cold. A handwritten announcement advertised a local cleanup along the canal. Someone had drawn a smiling fish beside the date.

I stopped long enough to read it.

The cleanup had been scheduled for the previous weekend.

I continued.

There was something oddly satisfying about moving through a town without having to reach anywhere in particular. The streets were not impressive. Most of the buildings were ordinary, and some were plainly in need of repair. But they belonged to people who had somewhere to be, things to maintain and errands that could not be postponed forever.

A woman carried two bags of groceries through a narrow gate while speaking to someone inside the house. A delivery driver reversed his small truck into a cramped yard under the direction of a man who seemed determined to make the process more difficult than necessary. An elderly couple argued over which side of the crossing had the better pavement.

I watched them for a moment.

Then I crossed behind them.

Near the northern canal basin, the pavement opened onto a broader road. A low pedestrian bridge crossed a drainage channel, connecting two residential blocks. I stopped halfway across to drink the coffee.

It was too sweet.

I finished it anyway.

From the bridge, I could see the academy ridge behind the rooftops. The spire was visible again, though now it appeared farther away, reduced to a narrow green point between two taller buildings.

I took my phone out and checked the time.

I had been walking for a while.

The morning had become warmer, but the wind remained cold where the road opened toward the water. I followed a side street past a row of older houses, then came out beside a small public square where a few children were riding bicycles in circles around a dry fountain.

A boy lost control of his front wheel and nearly struck a bench.

His father caught the handlebars before the bicycle tipped.

The boy immediately began explaining why it had not been his fault.

I moved on before the explanation reached its conclusion.

By the time I reached the northern edge of the developed district, I had spent several hours moving through Kuoh without having accomplished anything particularly important.

It was a good change.

The road began to curve toward the foothills. Industrial buildings stood farther apart here, separated by vacant lots and municipal service facilities. A pumping station occupied one corner behind a brick retaining wall. Beyond it, road-maintenance trucks were parked beside mounds of winter grit.

Past the last cluster of workshops, the pavement narrowed.

A wide band of municipal parkland ran along the base of the foothills, separating the developed basin from the forested slopes. Young cherry trees stood beside the road, their bare branches moving gently in the wind.

The greenbelt was visible from the road as an irregular line of dark cedar and cypress.

I could have turned back.

Instead, I followed the path.

---

The park was quieter than the neighbourhood behind it.

A few picnic tables stood on the brown grass of a meadow. Near the public resthouse, a gravel trail wound uphill toward the trees, passing wooden markers warning visitors about wild boars and loose ground.

I was approaching the first cedar grove when I heard a child crying.

It was the high, breathless kind of crying that followed a sudden fright. Not the exhausted wail of someone who had been hurt, but the furious, disoriented noise of a small person who had lost something important and did not understand why the world had allowed it.

A woman was kneeling beside a bench twenty yards ahead.

She wore a heavy navy cardigan and a canvas apron. One hand held a small boy by the elbow. The other was trying to keep a wicker pet carrier upright.

The carrier had tipped onto its side.

Its wire door swung loose from a broken hinge.

"Ren, stay here," the woman said. "Don't run toward the ditch."

The boy was wearing bright yellow rubber boots. His face was red, and he was twisting against her grip.

"Mii-chan!"

A small calico cat crouched on the gravel path ten paces beyond them. Its ears were flattened, and its tail moved in short, nervous jerks.

A motor scooter rattled across a steel cattle grid farther down the parkway.

The sound cracked through the quiet.

The cat sprang.

It cleared the drainage swale and disappeared beneath the cedar branches.

"Mii-chan!"

The boy threw himself forward.

The woman caught him around the chest before he could reach the slope. Her balance shifted, one knee sliding against the gravel.

"Ren! No!"

She pulled him back toward the bench.

The cat was gone.

I stopped walking.

The woman looked up at me. Her expression changed when she noticed the stranger. It was a small change, but familiar: the embarrassed, defensive look people sometimes wore when an ordinary problem became visible to someone outside the family.

"Did it go into the trees?" I asked.

"Behind the marker." She tightened her arm around the boy. "She's wearing a red collar. She's frightened of engines. I can't leave him here."

The boy was still crying.

"Please," the woman added, looking toward the cedar slope. "Don't go too far. The municipal path ends at the gulley."

I looked toward the trees.

There was a narrow strip of ground between the public trail and the first line of cedar trunks. I could see where the cat had crossed.

"I'll try to turn her back toward the meadow."

"Don't climb past the timber rails."

"I won't."

I stepped off the gravel.

It wasn't a heroic decision.

I had long legs, thick shoes and no immediate appointment. The cat was probably frightened rather than injured, and if I could get it moving in the right direction, the woman might be able to catch it before it disappeared again.

That was all.

I pushed through the roadside ferns.

The light changed almost immediately.

The canopy was dense enough to hide most of the sky, and the ground beneath it was covered in a thick layer of red-brown needles. Moss had gathered over the exposed roots, making the slope uneven underfoot.

I could still hear the parkway behind me.

The scooter's engine faded first. Then the wind moving through the cherry branches. The child's crying continued for several seconds before becoming difficult to distinguish from the other sounds around me.

I saw the cat on a shelf of grey shale beneath a fallen log.

Its white chest moved rapidly. The red collar was visible against its fur, but the little brass bell beneath its chin hung motionless.

"Hey."

The cat watched me.

"Come here."

It turned its head.

I crouched slightly, keeping my hands low.

"Your owner's having a terrible morning."

The cat blinked.

Then it slipped over the back of the shale shelf.

I followed.

The slope was steeper than it had looked from below. I used my hands against the damp rock to steady myself, careful not to put too much weight on the loose patches of moss.

The cat stayed within sight.

A flash of white and orange appeared between the trunks, stopped, then moved again.

I climbed over a low rock shelf and ducked beneath a cypress branch.

The sound of the parkway had disappeared.

Not faded.

Disappeared.

I stopped beneath the branch and listened.

There should have been something. The distant scooter, perhaps. The wind. A bird calling from the meadow.

Instead, I heard only the faint movement of my jacket and the uneven scrape of my shoes against the needles.

The forest was not silent. Small sounds continued around me. A twig clicked somewhere above my head. Something moved through the undergrowth farther down the slope.

But the open space beyond the trees had gone.

I looked back.

The path I had climbed was still there, though it seemed longer than it should have been. The cedar trunks behind me stood close together, their dark branches overlapping until the spaces between them became difficult to judge.

I took out my phone.

The screen lit up.

The clock was still visible, but the numbers flickered once before disappearing. The signal indicator had become a blank grey shape.

I waited.

Nothing changed.

I locked the phone and returned it to my pocket.

"Right."

My voice sounded wrong.

Not distorted. Not muffled.

Simply smaller.

The syllables seemed to stop a short distance from my face.

I looked toward the cat.

It was sitting on a mossy boulder roughly fifty feet ahead. Its ears were raised, and its head was turned toward something beyond the path.

I followed its gaze.

Between two trees, the slope continued upward. A narrow track ran along the hillside, its surface packed hard and free of fallen branches.

It had not been visible from below.

I looked back again.

The public path should have been somewhere behind me. I had not climbed for more than a few minutes, and the meadow should still have been visible through the trunks.

Instead, the ground fell away into a deep ravine.

The trees descended into a grey mist that swallowed their lower branches. The slope did not look steep enough to have produced such a drop.

I stood there for several seconds.

Then the cat moved.

I followed it.

Not because I was brave. Not because I had suddenly become committed to rescuing a cat at any cost.

Standing still on an unfamiliar slope was not going to make the situation easier.

The track curved around a shoulder of dark rock.

I passed a cluster of pale stones, then a tree whose roots had grown over the exposed edge of a buried concrete slab. The ground beneath my feet remained firm, but the slope seemed to change its angle whenever I looked back.

The cat kept its distance.

Once, I heard the little bell beneath its collar.

It rang twice.

The sound came from my left.

I turned.

There was nothing between the trunks except a dark strip of ground and the grey shapes of fallen branches.

When I looked forward again, the cat was farther up the track.

I continued.

---

The first sign that I was not alone came from the trees.

A branch moved behind me.

It was not the light trembling of leaves. The sound was low and deliberate, a short wooden creak followed by a pause.

I turned.

Nothing.

The slope behind me was empty.

I waited.

A second sound came from farther to the right.

Something had shifted its position.

I could not see it, but I had the uncomfortable impression that whatever had moved was no longer behind me.

I looked along the track.

The cat had stopped beside a boulder.

Its body was low. Its ears pointed toward the trees on my left.

"Come on."

It did not move.

I took a step.

The cat sprang forward and disappeared around the next bend.

I followed, but slower now.

The track widened as it entered a sunken hollow. The trees changed there. The cedars became thinner, their bark peeling away in long grey strips. Several stood completely dead, their branches reaching into the dull light like blackened fingers.

The ground was covered in pale grass that did not move.

I could feel a cold draft against my cheek.

The grass remained still.

I looked at it for a moment, then at the trees.

Something was wrong with the wind.

I could feel it moving across my skin, but it did not disturb the grass or the loose debris around my shoes.

A faint scrape came from the slope behind me.

I turned.

A narrow line of grey dust slid down the face of a boulder.

I watched it settle.

Then, from somewhere beyond the trees to my right, came a sound like a shoe being placed carefully on stone.

I could not see anyone.

The track bent toward a black rock face.

Beyond it stood a house.

It was an older two-storey structure, leaning slightly toward the ravine. The lower floor had heavy timber framing and the remains of sliding wooden shutters. The upper storey had been built in a different style, with weatherboards, narrow sash windows and a sagging porch roof supported by square cedar posts.

The building looked as if it had been repaired several times by people who had never agreed on how it should be done.

One corner of the roof had collapsed inward.

The front door hung crookedly on a single hinge.

The cat sat on the third porch step.

It was licking its front paw.

For a moment, I felt relieved.

"Found you."

The cat stopped licking.

It looked at me, then stood and stretched.

I reached the edge of the clearing.

The house was forty yards away, but the distance seemed difficult to judge. The front steps looked close enough to reach in a few seconds. The windows seemed much farther away.

I glanced behind me.

The dead trees stood around the hollow.

Nothing moved.

I took another step.

A soft impact came from the left side of the house.

Not a footstep.

Something heavier, landing on a wooden surface.

I stopped.

The cat walked through the six-inch gap in the front door.

"Don't go in there."

It disappeared into the hall.

I looked at the house.

The door was open. The cat was inside. The woman was waiting somewhere beyond the trees, and I had no idea how far I had wandered from the public trail.
