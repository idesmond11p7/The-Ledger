Chapter Seven — The Examination
The red string parted with a dry, fibrous snap.

The proctor didn't look up.

He opened the brown wrapping around the examination booklets, folded it back, and set it on the corner of the lectern. Beneath it were two stacks of grey-covered papers, each one marked with a black registration grid.

The younger teacher took one stack.

They split the room without speaking.

One went down the window side. The other took the wall. Their shoes made different sounds against the wooden floor as they moved between the rows.

At each desk, a booklet landed near the outer edge.

Slap.

Slap.

Slap.

The boy two seats to my left reached for his before the proctor had finished the row.

"Hands flat until distribution is complete."

The boy pulled his hand back.

A booklet landed in front of me.

I left it alone.

The answer sheet came next. It was stiff and off-white, divided into numbered columns of small boxes.

I kept both hands on the desk.

The wood was cool beneath my palms. The front edge had been worn smooth by years of use. There was a shallow mark beside the brass desk number, three rough strokes cut into the surface.

"You have three minutes to complete the administrative fields," the senior proctor said. "Use an HB pencil. Fill the response boxes completely. If your applicant number does not match your seating placard, raise your hand."

A chair scraped somewhere behind me.

I picked up my pencil.

The cedar casing felt cold between my fingers.

I pulled the blue placard closer.

The first box took the number easily.

Then the name.

Lowe, Ira.

My hand moved through the characters without much thought.

The phonetic guide came next.

The strange part was how little translation happened in my head.

I knew what I was writing. I also didn't have to stop and think about how to write it. My hand simply knew where the strokes went and how much pressure to use.

Jonah knew what the sounds meant.

The hand belonged to Ira.

For a few seconds, those two things existed at the same time.

Then I moved on.

Thirty-something pencils were working around me.

The room filled with the soft scratch of graphite.

"Pencils down."

The second hand reached the top of the clock.

"The morning session consists of two modules. Language and Mathematics. You will have eighty minutes. Turn your booklets over. Begin."

Pages turned.

I turned mine.

The first section was Language.

Forty questions covered grammar, vocabulary, kanji readings, and two longer passages.

Question one was easy.

So was two.

Three.

Four.

I worked through the first page without slowing down.

The questions weren't difficult yet. They were mostly checking whether you knew the material you were supposed to know.

I didn't have to translate every sentence into English before understanding it. The characters registered as meaning almost immediately.

That saved time.

By question fifteen, I had settled into a rhythm.

Read.

Recognize.

Choose.

Fill.

Turn the page.

The room was getting warmer.

The radiators beneath the windows had started clicking. Heat collected near the ceiling and settled back down over us.

A boy across the aisle took off his jacket and hung it over the back of his chair.

My sweater was beginning to feel too warm.

I loosened the top button of my shirt and went back to the booklet.

Section two was comprehension.

The first passage was about post-war urban planning in Kanagawa.

It wasn't difficult, but it was dense.

I reached the third paragraph and realized I had read the same sentence twice without keeping any of it.

I stopped.

An eraser was working somewhere to my right.

Scritch.

Scritch.

Scritch.

The sound continued for several seconds.

I looked sideways.

The boy beside me was erasing a large section of his answer sheet. He had both hands braced against the desk and was moving his arm hard enough to shake the bench.

My pencil moved slightly.

I lifted it.

The boy stopped.

Grey shavings covered the corner of his desk.

He brushed them away and bent over his paper again.

I looked back at the passage.

The question asked what the author considered the main problem with private railway expansion during the 1960s.

I read the relevant section again.

A was too broad.

C contradicted the second paragraph.

D used language that didn't fit the author's argument.

B.

I filled the answer.

That was better.

I kept going.

The last few language questions took longer, but I reached the end with time left.

Then I turned the page.

Mathematics.

The first problems were straightforward.

Quadratics.

Factorization.

Inequalities.

I worked them in the margins, keeping the steps short.

The pencil had started to dull.

I turned it once between my fingers and continued.

Question twenty-four stopped me.

It involved a coordinate plane, an equilateral triangle, a parabola, and a circle.

I read it once.

Then again.

I started substituting values.

The expression got worse.

I stopped writing.

Look at the diagram.

The triangle was equilateral.

One point was at the origin.

The symmetry gave me the rest.

I erased two lines and started again.

This time the answer came quickly.

I checked the coordinates.

Option C.

I filled the corresponding box.

"Twenty minutes remaining."

The room changed.

Pages began turning faster.

Someone dropped a pencil. It struck the floor twice before rolling underneath another desk.

I kept working.

Twelve questions remained.

Basic mechanics.

Stoichiometry.

Circuit diagrams.

My right hand hurt.

The muscles between my thumb and forefinger had tightened around the pencil. I lowered my hand beneath the desk and shook it once.

Then again.

The skin around my knuckle had gone red.

I picked the pencil up again.

Outside, the sky had cleared.

Sunlight came through the high windows and caught the white answer sheet badly enough that I had to shift it a little to the left.

Question thirty-eight.

Heat of vaporization.

Thirty-nine.

Chemical equilibrium.

Forty.

Wave velocity.

I reached the end with three minutes left.

I went back to the answer sheet.

Not to solve anything.

Just to check.

I ran down the numbers, looking for a skipped box or a double mark.

Nothing.

I turned the pencil around and used the eraser to correct one small mark near the bottom.

Then I put it down.

"Pencils down."

The room stopped.

"Hands flat on the desks. Cease all writing immediately. Any further writing will invalidate your examination."

Someone's pencil moved.

The proctor saw it.

"You."

He crossed the room.

The boy froze.

The proctor took the pencil from his hand and looked at the answer sheet.

"I gave the command."

The boy stared at the desk.

The proctor marked something on the form and moved on.

Nobody spoke.

We kept our hands flat.

The wall clock ticked.

The proctors collected the answer sheets first.

One row at a time.

Each sheet was counted before the next was taken.

When the assistant reached me, he lifted my answer sheet by the corner and added it to the stack.

Then came the booklets.

The last one disappeared into the bundle.

The senior proctor straightened the stack against the lectern.

"The examination is concluded."

He checked his watch.

"Results will be dispatched by registered post on Friday morning. Take all belongings with you. Do not leave waste on the desks."

He packed the papers into a leather briefcase.

The clasps closed.

"You are dismissed."

The room came back all at once.

Chairs scraped.

Benches moved.

People started talking.

Someone behind me was already complaining about question twenty-four.

I stayed seated for a few seconds.

My back hurt.

When I stood, my knees clicked.

I packed the pencils, sharpener, and eraser into my bag.

The eraser had gone dark at both ends.

I put my coat on.

The room felt too warm now that the exam was over.

I stepped into the corridor with everyone else.

The difference in temperature was immediate.

Cool air came through the high windows along the colonnade.

I took a breath.

Better.

The staircase was crowded.

Applicants moved down in groups, some talking about the exam, others already calling their parents.

At the lower gallery, a few families waited.

A mother handed her son a thermos.

Another had brought rice balls wrapped in foil.

Several students left together, still arguing over answers.

I kept walking.

Outside, the courtyard was full.

The sun was higher now, and the gravel shifted beneath hundreds of shoes.

I passed through the main gates.

Nobody stopped me.

I started down the hill.

The academy disappeared behind the walls after a few minutes.

The streets widened as I went.

The houses around the school were large and well kept. The roads were quiet. A gardener worked along one curb, sweeping leaves into a pile.

A crow watched from the wall.

A delivery truck came slowly uphill.

I kept going.

The town changed as the slope flattened.

The large houses gave way to shops.

The first row of storefronts sat close together, most of them only two or three floors high. Signs hung above narrow entrances. Bicycles were parked along the pavement.

A greengrocer had crates of winter vegetables outside.

A woman sprayed water over a row of daikon.

The next shop repaired bicycles.

An old man was working on a wheel, turning it slowly and listening to the spokes.

Traffic passed between them.

A bus stopped farther down the street.

People got off.

Nobody paid much attention to the students leaving Kuoh Academy.

I kept walking.

Then I smelled bread.

I stopped.

The bakery was small.

Its glass door was propped open, and a little brass bell hung above it.

I went inside.

An older woman stood behind the counter.

"Welcome."

"Hello."

I took a tray and tongs from the entrance.

There were curry buns, sweet rolls, melon pan, and a few plain rolls.

I chose two.

The curry bun was still warm.

The woman put them into paper sleeves.

"Careful with the curry," she said. "It just came out."

"Thank you."

"Two hundred and forty yen."

I paid and stepped back outside.

There was a small park across from the shop.

I sat on the edge of the low concrete wall and opened the first paper sleeve.

The bun was hotter than I expected.

I took a bite.

The filling was beef, onion, and carrots.

I ate more carefully after that.

The street kept moving.

A postal worker stopped outside a pharmacy.

Two girls in navy uniforms walked past with a bag of roasted chestnuts between them.

A bicycle went through the intersection.

A truck stopped.

A pedestrian waited for it.

Then crossed.

I finished the curry bun.

The second one went faster.

I folded the paper wrappers and dropped them into a nearby bin.

Then I stood.

The exam was behind me now.

Nothing else had happened.

That was fine.

I checked the time and headed toward the station.

Kuoh Station was quieter than it had been that morning.

The same roof stretched over the platforms, but the morning crowd was gone.

I bought a ticket and went through.

A train was waiting.

I got into the rear carriage.

There were only a few people inside.

An older woman slept beside the doors with a shopping cart beside her.

Two junior-high boys were playing something on a handheld game system.

I took a window seat.

The bag rested against my legs.

The train moved.

Kuoh passed outside the glass.

The academy hill disappeared first.

Then the older houses.

Then the shops.

The train crossed the same roads I had seen from the ground earlier.

The crossing signals came down.

Cars waited.

The train passed.

The scenery gradually became more crowded.

Apartment blocks appeared.

Concrete walls.

Utility poles.

More wires.

By the time I changed trains, the carriage was much fuller.

I stood near the doors for most of the journey.

My hand still hurt.

There was graphite on the side of my palm.

I rubbed it with my thumb.

It left a grey mark across the skin.

I would know on Friday.

Until then, there wasn't much to do about it.

I watched the stations pass.

Eventually I reached my neighborhood.

The afternoon was already beginning to turn toward evening.

Shop signs were lit.

A dry cleaner handed a wrapped suit to a man waiting outside.

A food cart had started cooking near the intersection.

I turned the corner.

The apartment building looked the same as it had that morning.

Grey concrete.

Rows of doors.

The third-floor balcony.

I climbed the outside stairs.

The metal steps rang beneath my boots.

On the third floor, the wind was colder.

I took the key from my pocket and unlocked the door.

The lock turned with a heavy click.

I stepped inside.

"I'm back."

The words came out before I thought about them.

There was nobody there to answer.

I took off my shoes.

Then my coat.

I hung it behind the door and put my bag beside the low table.

The apartment was cold.

I went into the washroom and turned on the tap.

The water was colder than I expected.

I washed my hands.

Grey water ran into the drain.

I worked the soap into the side of my palm until the graphite was gone.

Then I looked up.

Ira was still in the mirror.

His hair had come loose during the train ride.

Dark strands hung around his face and over his shoulders.

His eyes looked tired.

I stared at him for a few seconds.

Then I reached up and gathered the hair behind my neck.

I tied it back.

"Good enough."

I left the washroom.

In the kitchen, I filled the kettle and set it on the burner.

The gas caught with a quiet hiss.

I sat beside the low table.

The blue examination placard was still in my bag.

I took it out and placed it beside the application papers.

I looked at the number once.

Then I put it away.

Outside, the first streetlights came on.

Their light reached through the balcony blind and fell across the wall.

The kettle began to heat.

I leaned back against the wall and closed my eyes.

For the first time since morning, I had nothing I needed to do.

I listened to the water beginning to boil.
