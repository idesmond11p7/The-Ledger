Chapter 4 — Beyond
For a while, there was nothing.

Not darkness. Darkness would have been something—it would have implied a room waiting quietly in the shadows, or an overcast sky, or at least the faint, comforting sense of space stretching away into the dark.

There was none of that.

There was simply no sensation of being anywhere at all.

Then awareness returned.

I couldn't feel my body.

That was the first thing that drifted back into focus. There was no weight beneath me, no mattress or floor pressing against my back, no rhythm of air filling lungs. I couldn't feel my hands, my feet, or the temperature of the air. I couldn't even tell whether I was facing upward or downward, or if those directions even meant anything anymore.

I was conscious, and that was the only thing I possessed.

And then, without warning, the dark opened.

Stars filled everything.

For a long time, I couldn't do anything except stare.

They weren't the quiet, shimmering pinpricks I remembered seeing from suburban backyards. From Earth, stars had always seemed gentle—little flecks of diamond dusted across an ink-black dome, beautiful because they were too far away to hurt.

Out here, distance offered no shelter.

They burned with terrifying vitality. Some blazed blue-white and fierce, their light so dense and immediate it felt almost physical. Others smoldered deep ochre and crimson through curtains of interstellar gas that drifted across the expanse, vast sheets of glowing dust with neither beginning nor end. There were nurseries where newborn stars clustered so thickly that the black between them vanished into sheets of pearl, and beyond them, entire galaxies turned through the silence like glowing wheels.

The longer I drifted, the less the word sky made any sense.

There was no sky.

There was only an immensity that refused to stop opening.

Something drifted across my field of view.

At first, I thought it was a dark cloud cutting across the backdrop of distant galaxies. Then the light behind it reappeared, untouched, and the dizzying truth settled in: the void hadn't shifted at all.

I had.

Or something had moved me.

Direction had already lost its grip on me, dissolving into pure perspective.

Then the light ahead bent.

It was subtle at first—just a few background stars bowing out of place, their positions smearing across the dark like oil on water.

Then the distortion widened into a full, unbroken curve.

A ring of light flared into existence.

And in the center—

nothing.

I knew what it was.

A black hole.

I had seen pictures, artistic renderings, CGI models in science documentaries with soothing narrators explaining event horizons and photon spheres. But none of those sleek graphics prepared me for the visceral wrongness of the real thing.

Seeing it without an atmosphere or a computer monitor between us was terrifying.

The blackness at its core wasn't a shadow. It was an absolute cancellation. Starlight striking the outer boundary simply died, while the surrounding field was dragged along its edge in warped, blinding arcs. The accretion disk twisted the logic of sight: the far edge arched up over the dark center, glowing like an impossible halo, while the underside wrapped beneath it. Light that should have traveled straight had been forced into circles, bent by a mass so heavy the fabric of reality seemed to sag beneath it.

It didn't look like a physical object.

It looked like a hole torn cleanly out of the universe.

And as I drifted farther, I realized it was only one of many.

Beyond it lay depths that made the singularity look like an afterthought. Stars that had seemed colossal shrank into embers. Galaxies blurred into pale smudges. Structures so vast I lacked the language to describe them rose from the dark, hung suspended, and then dwindled into specks against whatever lay further out.

The universe wasn't growing.

I was just finally far enough away to realize how small my imagination had always been.

It should have broken me.

Instead, a strange, hollow wonder took over.

I had spent seventeen years looking up at the sky, convinced I understood what space meant.

I didn't know anything at all.

Then the framework collapsed.

There was no sound, no flash, no threshold to mark where one reality ended and the next began.

The rules that kept things apart simply stopped working.

A shape unfolded in front of me.

I called it a shape only because my mind had nothing else to reach for. Its outer surface folded smoothly inward and somehow emerged behind itself. Another structure intersected it without a collision, sliding through solid faces without leaving a tear. A line extended away from me and returned from the opposite direction, arriving behind my perspective without ever turning a corner.

I tried tracking a single edge just to anchor myself.

I couldn't.

Every time I thought I had grasped where it led, the planes rearranged themselves.

Inside became outside.

Distant points became near.

A boundary that should have closed something off ended up holding the empty space around it. I couldn't tell if the geometry was folding in front of me, or if my own mind was being bent to fit an impossible angle.

Then everything compressed.

Not by shrinking.

Things simply moved closer together while keeping their scale. Distances squeezed into zero space, forcing near and far into the exact same coordinates. For a breathless moment, there was nowhere left to exist because everywhere was happening at once.

Then the tension snapped.

The impossible forms drifted apart, loosening their grip, and faded into the dark.

I kept moving.

How long it lasted, I couldn't say.

Time eventually became impossible to hold onto.

At the beginning, I tried counting. Seconds, minutes, hours—anything to create a rhythm. But the numbers lost their weight. Hours blurred into stretches that felt like decades. I remember waiting. I remember the heavy, hollow ache of being entirely alone. I remember the creeping numbness that comes when you realize there is nothing left to react to.

Yet if someone had demanded an accounting of those years, I wouldn't have had a single memory to offer.

There was no yesterday.

No morning.

No evening.

No markers on a wall.

Only an endless stretch of before and after, pulled so thin that the difference evaporated completely.

I was there for lifetimes.

Or maybe a few seconds.

Eventually, even the passage of time stopped registering.

Then the light went out.

The blackness that followed was distinct from everything that had preceded it.

There was nothing waiting behind it. No dormant stars hiding in the distance. No nebular dust, no outlines, no horizon.

Just absence.

It wasn't a peaceful dark. It pressed against me, thick and suffocating.

I tried to breathe, forgetting that I had no chest to expand. There was no ribcage to tighten, no skin for cold to prickle, no pulse to hammer against my temples.

Yet the panic came anyway.

I wanted out.

Then, a thread of light caught my eye.

It started faint, a thin ribbon of pale blue drifting in the far distance. Then violet bled into it, followed by jade, and then an incandescent, liquid gold. The colors wove into currents, broad rivers of radiance coursing through the void as if an entire hidden ocean had suddenly decided to ignite.

It was breathtaking.

After so much emptiness, I wanted nothing more than to drift into it and stay there.

The currents began to pick up speed.

I didn't accelerate—I had no body to propel. But the space between us simply vanished. The ribbons lengthened, smearing into long streaks that erased the line between movement and illumination.

Then speed ceased to be an adequate word.

I was being carried somewhere.

Far beyond light. Far beyond anything humanity had ever measured or named.

And then, looking back across the distance, I saw it.

A solitary sphere.

Pale, blue-white, and impossibly fragile.

It took a few heartbeats for understanding to catch up.

The observable universe.

I stared back at it, transfixed.

It was so small.

Everything I had ever known was tucked inside that little marble.

Earth.

The sun.

Every star humans had ever mapped or written songs about.

Every street I had walked down, my high school, my home.

My mother.

Daniel.

Every single life, memory, and piece of history had played out inside that tiny, glowing bubble.

And I was slipping away from it.

I tried to reach out. I tried to arrest the drift, to anchor myself to anything.

Nothing answered.

The sphere shrank, dwindling into a solitary point of light in an ocean of unfamiliar dark.

The terror hit me then—sharp, raw, and desperate.

There was no heart to race in my chest. No throat to close up, no lungs to hitch, no hands to claw at the void.

And yet I was terrified.

The contradiction made it worse. How could terror exist when there was nothing left of me to be afraid?

I didn't know. I only knew that I wanted to go back.

The speck vanished.

Ahead, another boundary rose to meet me.

It didn't glow. It carried no discernible form or geometry. It possessed only an absolute, ancient presence, standing like a wall erected before the beginning of things.

I drew closer.

It felt heavy—not with mass, but with the quiet inevitability of something that had always existed and never needed to justify itself.

I met the edge.

For a fractured instant, everything hummed.

Space, time, identity, every memory of who I was and where I had been—all of it tangled together into a single, blinding point of tension.

Then—

BOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOM—

The universe shattered into light.

It wasn't a sound, but it struck harder than any thunderclap could have. Sensation overloaded entirely, burning out thought, sight, and self.

Then, silence.

And then—

the smell of damp earth.

Cold air.

The rough prickle of grass.

My eyes snapped open.

Above me, resting against the dark, was a moon.

Full. Pale. Familiar.

I was lying on a grassy hill next to an empty road.

For a long time, I stayed completely still, listening.

Crickets stirred in the tall grass nearby. Farther off, deep in a line of trees, an owl called into the evening. A chill breeze moved across the slope, bending the blades of grass until they brushed against my bare arms.

A car drove past along the asphalt below.

Its headlights washed over the slope, caught the gravel shoulder for a few seconds, and disappeared around a bend.

I watched the red taillights fade until the night reclaimed the asphalt.

The sheer normality of it made my head spin.

After where I had been, the hill felt claustrophobic. The road looked toy-like. The moon looked like a prop hung on a wire, entirely too close to the ground. The whole world felt as though it had been shrunk down to fit inside a box.

I shut my eyes.

The dark behind my lids hitched.

I snapped them open.

The moon was still there, silver and calm.

I closed them again.

There it was. For a microsecond, the blackness behind my eyes twisted, as if the reality in front of me had failed to render a frame. Not a picture, not a lingering afterimage. Just a tiny tear in the fabric of things.

I opened my eyes. Nothing.

I shut them.

It happened again.

And again.

Each time, the stutter grew fainter, smoothing out until the dark was just dark again.

Slowly, I pushed myself up.

That was when the first true wrongness hit.

My body.

I lifted a hand, turning it under the moonlight.

It was a hand. Five fingers, pale skin, neatly trimmed nails. It flexed when I wanted it to flex.

That should have been reassuring.

It wasn't.

The motion had been too fluid, executing a fraction of a heartbeat before I had fully finished willing it. It was like wearing gloves tailored so precisely they felt like a second skin, yet entirely alien beneath the surface.

I stood up.

My weight shifted, my balance correcting itself instantly with an effortless, predatory grace that wasn't mine.

I took one step, then another.

The body moved with quiet confidence, adjusting to the slope of the hill faster than my thoughts could track.

I stopped.

My gaze snapped to the treeline.

Then down the road to the drainage ditch.

Then to the blind corner where the road curved toward a cluster of distant buildings.

I stood frozen on the grass, my pulse steady, my breathing slow.

Why did I just check those?

I hadn't thought about escape routes or cover. The assessment had simply occurred, clean and instinctive, before I had even realized I was looking.

I forced myself to start walking again.

With every yard, the disconnect grew quieter, settling into the muscle and bone until the mechanics felt seamless. Not familiar. Never familiar. Just natural.

I drew a breath through my teeth and tried to speak.

“Hello?”

The sound broke the night, and I froze.

That wasn't my voice.

It was softer. A higher pitch, smooth and edged with a cadence I didn't recognize.

My throat worked, swallowing hard.

“Hello?” I tried again.

The tone modulated slightly, warming, adjusting to the shape of the mouth.

“Okay,” I whispered.

The word came out smoother this time, settling into a distinct, foreign register, as if the vocal cords were remembering how to function after a long silence.

I stared down at the asphalt, my chest tightening.

“What the hell...?”

Hearing those syllables carried by someone else's voice made my stomach drop.

I had to get off the road. I needed four walls, a roof, anywhere private where I could sit down and pull my mind back together.

I followed the road until it reached a three-story residential building. The outer gate sat unlatched, swinging slightly on its hinge, and the front door yielded with a quiet click when I turned the handle.

Inside, the air was still and cool.

An apartment.

It was dark, lit only by the amber glow of a streetlamp cutting through the sheer curtains.

It was empty, but it wasn't abandoned.

A ceramic mug sat near the sink with a thin ring dried at the bottom. A jacket hung over the back of a kitchen chair. A low shelf held paperbacks with creased spines, and small, everyday knick-knacks sat on the side table with the casual disarray of a life temporarily stepped away from.

Someone lived here.

I walked softly down the narrow hall, my socks making almost no sound against the wood.

There were no answers resting on the counter. No driver's license left out, no mail stacked with a name and an address, no framed family photos smiling from the wall. Just the quiet, lingering imprint of an interrupted routine.

At the end of the short corridor, I reached the bathroom.

The door stood open.

A tall mirror hung above the sink, catching the faint, bluish ambient light from the high window.

I stopped.

My mind locked up, refusing for several long seconds to assemble the visual data in front of me.

The figure in the glass looked back.

It stood exactly where I stood. It wore the clothes I was wearing. Its chest rose and fell in time with my own breath.

It wasn't Jonah.

I stared into the glass.

The unfamiliar, arresting eyes stared back from beneath strands of hair that weren't mine, framing a face I had never seen before in my life.

My jaw went slack.

“What the fuck?”
