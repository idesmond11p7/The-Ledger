## 2. Recovered central mental model

The research began as a response to additive, static description: prose that inventories surfaces, colors, temperatures, and objects while narrative time effectively stops.

The central hypothesis recovered by the Researcher:

> Hyper-immersive fiction creates the impression of a life that continues beyond the reader’s immediate attention.

The practical objective is to make the reader reconstruct an unfolding, materially coherent environment through selective information, embodied action, changing attention, and consequences. The world should not appear to exist only when the protagonist looks at it.

This connects the recovered methods:
- **Tool transparency:** skilled use foregrounds the task and consequences rather than turning the tool into an inventory.
- **Spatial planes:** maintain near, middle, and far information so the environment has depth and scale.
- **Environmental continuity:** background activity continues while foreground action occurs.
- **Acoustic dynamics:** foreground events can mask background sound; the wider soundscape can return when the foreground event ends.
- **Selective reconstruction:** salient anchors let readers infer a larger environment without exhaustive description.
- **Spatial traversal:** the place is revealed through movement, orientation, obstruction, and route choice.
- **Sentence and paragraph rhythm:** linguistic form contributes to the timing and force of action and attention.

These are interacting methods, not a checklist to apply mechanically to every paragraph.

### 3.1 Battlefield engagement — three-plane depth and tool transparency

> The berm gave him three feet of churned mud and crushed concrete to work with. He drove his chest into the slope, the bipod digging in, the world opening up across the river valley: two hundred meters of ruined industrial flats, crossed by burning fuel trucks and the steady, flat-line arc of red tracers stitching the overcast sky.
>
> Three silhouettes broke from the blown-out corner of the rail depot at sixty yards, sprinting low for a rusted shipping container.
>
> He didn't think about the rifle; the glass of the optic simply found the lead runner’s chest. He pressed the trigger twice. Two heavy punches into his right shoulder—crack-crack—and downrange, two sparks snapped off the runner’s chest plate. The man didn't stumble; he folded violently at the waist and hit the wet gravel face-first.

The specimen continues through three targets, then releases the trigger and restores the wider valley soundscape.

**Researcher's forensic self-critique:** (1) armor sparks followed by immediate collapse resemble game hit-marker feedback; (2) a weapon conveniently slipping from view acts as cinematic confirmation of a kill; (3) perceiving rounds passing inches overhead exceeds plausible sensory access; (4) the distant valley seems paused during the engagement and resumes afterward.

**Retained lesson:** foreground action should not erase the larger world; tool use should be rendered through task, feedback, and consequence. The specimen's critique is as important as its successful effects.

### 3.2 Mundane non-combat stress test — environmental continuity

> The bottom of the fourth box gave out before he cleared the threshold. Wet pulp tore down the center seam, and a stack of warped repair manuals hit the oily concrete with a heavy, sodden slap, fanning across his work boots.
>
> Across the alley, the body shop’s compressor kicked on with a dull, rhythmic thumping that vibrated through the gravel under his heels.
>
> “Don't try to lift them by the tape,” Miller said. He didn't look up from the workbench; he was scraping dried gasket compound off an aluminum head with a putty knife, his strokes short and methodical. “The moisture’s gotten into the corrugation. Drag them onto the tarp.”

The specimen continues with a partly obscured loading lane, glare, an approaching vehicle heard before it is seen, drizzle at the eaves, a distant shunt-engine horn, and Miller resuming his work.

**Demonstrated effects:** material consequence; active background sound; a character continuing a separate task; visual obstruction by glare; sound changing with distance and architecture; a wider environment continuing beyond the immediate exchange.

**Retained lesson:** the method must work in ordinary scenes, not only combat or spectacle. The world remains active while the viewpoint character attends to a local problem.

### 3.3 Municipal traversal — iterative revision receipts

The Researcher recovered four versions of an exercise in which Ira reaches a junction, identifies a bathhouse chimney using a station map, discovers a road obstruction, and chooses another route. This scenario is an exercise specimen, **not established Chapter 9 geography or canon**.

**Version 1 — information architecture:** establishes the climbing lane, junction, bathhouse, intended canal route, barriers, crane, trench, and alternate street. It was assessed as spatially informative but detached and inventory-like.

**Version 2 — psychological narration:** introduces Ira's suspicion that he missed a turn, recognition of the bathhouse, relief, time calculation, annoyance, and uncertainty about the alternate route. It was assessed as more viewpoint-aware but over-explanatory in its map arithmetic.

**Version 3 — integrated baseline:**

> The lane kept climbing between concrete retaining walls long enough that Ira began to suspect he had missed his turn. Only when the grade finally leveled into a four-way junction did the square brick chimney of the ward bathhouse rise above the shuttered storefronts opposite—right where the station map had promised it would be.
>
> He let out a breath and rounded the corner toward the downhill road that led to the canal bridge.
>
> Fifty yards down the slope, orange barriers blocked both lanes. A flatbed crane sat idling beside an open trench in the asphalt, its steel outriggers planted across the sidewalk where the pedestrian passage should have been.

This version integrates route orientation, expectation, relief, obstruction, and movement more successfully, though sightline and physical plausibility still require scrutiny.

**Version 3.1 — attempted calibration:**

> The lane kept climbing between concrete retaining walls long enough that Ira began to suspect he had missed his turn. Only when the grade finally leveled into a four-way junction did the square brick chimney of the ward bathhouse rise above the shuttered storefronts opposite—matching the blue mark on the station flyer.
>
> He let out a breath and swung right around the retaining wall, aiming for the downhill road toward the canal bridge.
>
> The low, wet clatter of an idling diesel hit him the moment his shoulder cleared the masonry.
>
> Fifty yards down the slope, orange barriers blocked both lanes. A flatbed crane occupied the center of the asphalt beside an open trench, its steel outriggers extended sideways and driven flat into the sidewalk where the pedestrian path should have been.

This version tries to ground the turn, sound arrival, and gaze reorientation. It is not automatically superior. “Low, wet clatter” may be an ill-fitting sound description; “driven flat into the sidewalk” is mechanically unclear; exhaust masking canal water confuses sensory channels unless physically justified; and “secondary culvert” may be too technical for Ira. Replacing map personification with “matching the blue mark” is optional, not a universal correction.

**Retained lesson:** revision should improve the reader's understanding of where the viewpoint character is, what they notice, what obstructs the intended route, and why they choose an alternative. Added technical precision is not necessarily clearer or more natural prose.

## 5. Methodological corrections and superseded rules

The report's final five operational commandments contain useful instincts but overstate them as absolutes:

- **Anchor before movement:** useful when orientation matters, not a mandatory sentence order.
- **Non-Euclidean fatigue:** an evocative label, not a literal rule. Distance can be experienced through effort and expectation; objective measurements are not forbidden.
- **Acoustic integration:** sound should be physically plausible. Sound does not universally arrive before visual confirmation.
- **Preserve the horizon:** the wider world should feel active beyond the immediate task, without requiring constant background reminders.
- **Tool transparency:** useful when a character is skilled and the tool is subordinate to the action. It should not prevent attention to the tool when its operation is narratively important.

The later revised Topic 3 specification supersedes earlier dogmatic versions:
- Sound and sight order depends on geometry, line of sight, and the environment.
- Not every gaze shift requires a narrated body pivot.
- Obstacle-clearance detail is needed when it matters to movement or decision.
- Emotion labels are allowed when efficient and supported by context.
- Prose modes are fluid ranges, not rigid scene categories.
- Sensory impressions do not establish underlying fictional mechanics.

## 6. Three vocabulary registers

Retain the distinction as a practical teaching device:
1. **Analytical register:** terminology used by researchers and reviewers to diagnose prose.
2. **Pedagogical register:** plain, actionable instructions to the Writer.
3. **Narrative register:** natural, character-appropriate prose.

Technical terminology should not enter narrative merely to display the research. Equally, do not flatten sophisticated prose into artificially simple language. The target is natural expression, not a vocabulary ban.

## 7. Current synthesis for future Writer training

1. **Construct a reconstructable environment.** Give readers enough stable anchors to understand where events occur and how the environment relates to the viewpoint character.
2. **Keep the world active.** Background events and environmental processes need not stop when foreground action intensifies.
3. **Control attention.** Decide what the character notices, misses, misunderstands, or recognizes later.
4. **Render action through consequence.** Physical actions should alter position, objects, surfaces, sound, or available choices.
5. **Use selective detail.** Choose details that establish orientation, material behavior, atmosphere, action, or meaningful contrast; do not inventory everything.
6. **Preserve sensory causality.** Light, sound, surfaces, distance, and obstruction should behave consistently with the scene.
7. **Let language carry time and movement.** Syntax, stress, punctuation, paragraphing, and repetition can shape pace and attention.
8. **Revise against reader experience.** Technical precision is not necessarily clarity. Test sightlines, material plausibility, sensory channels, and viewpoint naturalness.

This is a working synthesis of recovered receipts, not a closed or exhaustive standard.
