# 4. THE PROSE STANDARD: READABLE, DENSELY EXPERIENCED, AND REJECTION-FIRST

## 4.1 First-pass readability is not the same as simplicity

A reader should generally understand what is happening on the first pass. Avoid needlessly obscure syntax, opaque pronouns, unexplained technical vocabulary, inflated terminology, tangled clause structures, and paragraphs that require rereading merely to identify who did what.

However, do **not** respond by making the prose shallow, short, or mechanically plain. The target is dense experiential digestion: a passage may be immediately understandable while also letting the reader simulate several connected facts at once.

A physical action can establish spatial position, reveal a habit, change the soundscape, expose a practical limitation, and alter what the narrator can see. Let those consequences coexist when the scene supports them. Do not unpack every consequence into a separate explanatory sentence.

**Target:** maximum experiential value with minimum unnecessary mediation.

## 4.2 Productive rereading versus failed clarity

Productive rereading is welcome. A reader may return to a paragraph because it contains spatial relationships, a subtle behavioral tell, an implication that becomes clearer after a later scene, or a dense moment worth experiencing again.

Unproductive rereading is a failure. If the reader must reread because the prose obscured the subject, muddled the sequence, buried the point in abstractions, or made a simple action difficult to parse, repair it.

Do not remove useful complexity simply because it rewards attention. Do not preserve confusing writing merely because it looks literary.

## 4.3 Demonstrate before interpreting

When an action, object, spatial relation, pause, or exchange already communicates something, do not append a sentence that paraphrases its meaning.

For example, if Ira turns the placard facedown, discovers that the examination remains unchanged, and puts it away, do not then explain the same idea three different ways. Keep an interpretation only if it adds a new layer: a contradiction, an unflattering self-recognition, a changed decision, or a consequence the physical action alone could not carry.

Reject the common AI pattern:

1. Show a physical action.
2. State what the action means.
3. Restate the emotional meaning.
4. Summarize the theme.
5. End the paragraph with a polished aphorism.

That pattern converts experience into a guided tour of its own significance. The reader should be allowed to make reasonable inferences.

## 4.4 The narrator is allowed to think, but not to narrate his own thesis constantly

Ira is analytical and prone to rehearsing possible outcomes. His internal reasoning is essential. It should not become a stream of generic wisdom about uncertainty, rejection, life, or human nature.

Let thoughts arise from concrete triggers: an unavailable job, a missing local reference, a question from a stranger, an instruction from a shopkeeper, the route he chooses, the time he checks, or the terms of Kenji's offer.

Keep the thought attached to the event that caused it. A brief thought can be enough. If the scene already carries the insight, do not make Ira explain it again.

## 4.5 Reject fake profundity and artificial closure

Do not end every scene with a maxim. Do not force the chapter to announce what the reader should learn. Avoid elegant-sounding conclusions that exceed the character's actual progress.

The ending should not declare that Ira has defeated his old habits, found himself, become independent, or stopped waiting. He has accepted one errand. Tomorrow's appointment is a real change in his immediate schedule, not proof of a permanent psychological breakthrough.

## 4.6 Avoid these habits unless a specific instance is earned

- Repeated emotional labels that merely rename the same feeling.
- Repeated formulations of “I could go home,” “I had no reason,” “that was enough,” or equivalent verbal tics.
- Mechanical descriptions of every footstep, turn, and door opening.
- Generic atmospheric prose that could be transferred unchanged to any town or character.
- Dialogue where every speaker sounds unusually articulate, perfectly composed, or designed to deliver the theme.
- Symmetrical rhetorical structures repeated until they become a visible template.
- Excessive fragments used to manufacture intensity.
- Repetitive contrast formulas such as “not X, but Y” when no genuine distinction needs emphasis.
- Explaining a social interaction after the dialogue and then explaining it again in the narrator's next thought.
- An automatic sentence that converts every mundane detail into a metaphor.
- Artificially elevated vocabulary where a direct word would be more accurate.
- Repeated declarations that a result is “ordinary,” “useful,” “reasonable,” “practical,” or “manageable” without a new concrete reason.
- Repeated negative lists of everything Ira cannot do.
- Excessive self-analysis that makes a brief encounter feel like a formal therapy session.
- Unnecessary validation from strangers.
- Characters who exist solely to teach Ira a lesson.
- A protagonist who is subtly made superior by every person's reaction.
- An ending sentence that summarizes the chapter's moral instead of allowing the final action to carry it.

This is a rejection-first standard. A sentence does not deserve to remain merely because it is grammatical, vivid, polished, or thematically relevant. It must perform a distinct function in the scene.

# 5. IRA'S PSYCHOLOGY: THE ACTUAL CHANGE MUST BE SMALL AND CAUSAL

Ira is not merely “anxious.” The chapter should make his particular pattern visible through the decisions he makes.

He can tolerate tasks with defined procedures. An examination gives him questions, time limits, answer spaces, and a point at which he must submit. Once the examination is over, he has no further action available that can affect the result. Waiting removes the feedback loop he knows how to use.

He responds by trying to manufacture another task. Looking for work is materially sensible because money is limited. It also supplies an immediate destination, a reason to leave the apartment, and a series of small actions that can be completed. The motive should remain mixed. Do not reduce the job search to a metaphor or pretend it is only a psychological exercise.

His defensive pattern is this:

1. He considers a possible action.
2. He anticipates a plausible obstacle or refusal.
3. Because the obstacle is plausible, he starts treating it as though it has already happened.
4. He constructs a negative conclusion about his chances or suitability.
5. That conclusion lets him withdraw without exposing himself to the actual answer.
6. When he catches himself doing this, he may continue anyway, but the recognition does not instantly eliminate the habit.

The distinction matters: **a real refusal is information; an imagined refusal is not yet evidence.** Ira must learn to notice the difference through experience, not through a lecture.

Do not make every rejection emotionally devastating. Most business owners are busy, and their refusals are ordinary. The psychological pressure comes from what Ira starts adding to those refusals in his own mind.

Do not make every refusal secretly kind or encouraging. The shopkeepers need not reward his effort. They can be neutral, hurried, unavailable, or matter-of-fact. Their reactions should have causes grounded in their work.

Do not have Ira diagnose his entire personality in one polished paragraph. The reader should see the pattern recur in different contexts, with the narrator's recognition becoming more precise over time.

## 5.1 His previous life and social avoidance

A brief memory of online hostility and withdrawn conversations may help explain why he anticipates rejection before it occurs. Use this material only where it organically connects to the present situation. Do not dump his entire former life into the chapter. Do not invent an exact incident, person, timeline, or trauma that the project has not established.

The remembered pattern is enough: conversations could turn hostile, remarks could be stripped of context, and Ira sometimes stopped replying or left before another exchange could become painful. That habit once protected him in some circumstances; it later became difficult to distinguish prudent withdrawal from automatic avoidance.

Do not use this memory to imply that all people are cruel or that ordinary social interaction is inherently dangerous.

## 5.2 No instant cure

By the end, Ira has not become socially fluent, confident, fearless, or cured of defensive thinking. He has recognized one useful distinction and made one limited choice despite uncertainty. He can still worry tomorrow, second-guess himself, or encounter a problem he did not predict.

His progress must be behavioural and observable: he continues asking; he gathers information; he evaluates the errand's actual terms; he accepts it; he records the appointment and prepares what he can.

# 6. REQUIRED CHAPTER STRUCTURE AND CAUSAL SEQUENCE

The following sequence is the intended architecture. It is not a demand to preserve every line of the source draft. The writer may combine, trim, rearrange within a scene, or deepen an event if doing so strengthens causality and continuity. Do not remove a major beat without a compelling reason.

## Movement I — Tuesday evening: the examination has ended, but waiting has begun

Open with the kettle beginning to murmur. Ira reaches to turn down or extinguish the flame before it boils fully. Let the sound and the small physical action give the opening a real domestic location.

There is graphite beneath his fingernails. He has washed his hands after returning from Kuoh Academy, but a faint gray line remains in the creases of his palm. The examination is still present through residue, not a flashback that replays the entire day.

The placard marked **1044** lies on the table. Its cardboard has a slight curl or crease from being handled. Ira picks it up, checks the number even though he knows it, and puts it down. The object once had a clear function: it identified his examination place and connected him to a booklet and a result. At home it no longer gives him an action to perform.

The evening outside is ordinary: a narrow strip of sky between neighbouring buildings, a bicycle or vehicle passing, the sound fading around a corner. Use a few accurate sensory details rather than a generic catalogue of atmosphere.

Ira remembers the examination through specific procedural sensations: graphite against paper, the desk against his forearms, the proctor's voice, the final warning, the sequence of answering and checking. Do not reproduce the entire exam or assert how well he performed.

The important contrast: during the examination, there was always another task. Now the result cannot be altered by reviewing it. The result is expected by registered post on Friday morning.

He may revisit an answer in his mind, but he recognizes that reconstructing it cannot change the submitted paper. The thought should feel like an unwanted mental loop, not an essay about uncertainty.

He turns the placard facedown or otherwise puts it away. He stores it in the drawer with ordinary household clutter, where it no longer occupies the centre of the table. This does not solve the waiting. It is a small action that changes what he is looking at, not what has happened.

He goes to bed after checking the lock. The repeated check can indicate restlessness without turning him into a caricature of obsessive behaviour. Avoid an extended ending monologue.

**Scene purpose:** establish the loss of task structure after the examination. The chapter starts with a person who has completed what he can control and is struggling to tolerate the empty interval.

## Movement II — Wednesday morning: making a day for himself

Ira wakes a little after seven. It is late February, and the air outside the blanket is cool. The apartment is materially unchanged. Nothing occurred overnight that resolves the examination or demands an immediate response.

He makes a simple breakfast from what he has. He hears a delivery truck pass outside and notices himself listening until the sound fades. Do not over-symbolize the truck.

He cleans up, checks the time, and counts his money. The starting total is approximately **¥9,840**. The count has not changed since the previous evening. His finances matter, but do not repeat the amount in every scene.

He considers staying home. There are books, chores, and ways to organize the apartment. The option is genuinely defensible: it saves money and avoids pointless travel. The flaw is not that rest is inherently bad. The flaw is that he can use a reasonable argument to justify almost any withdrawal if he selectively ignores what it costs him.

He lays out his coat, checks his wallet and keys, considers a notebook, puts it back, and hesitates. Use these small decisions to make the departure feel effortful. Do not mechanically narrate every dressing action.

He decides to look for temporary work. This is materially sensible because money is finite. It also gives the day a destination. Keep both motives alive.

He has no local references and cannot promise indefinite availability. Those are real disadvantages. He should know they might matter, but they should not become a prewritten verdict that makes trying pointless.

He leaves the apartment and heads toward the station. The transition from the indoor stillness to the colder, active street should register through his body and attention.

**Scene purpose:** Ira chooses an action without knowing whether it will work. The choice is modest, not triumphant.

## Movement III — Kuoh's lower town: let geography shape the search

The academy sits on higher ground. The lower commercial and industrial districts connect through the station approaches, railway embankment, underpass, canal roads, covered arcade, residential streets, and service lanes.

The reader should be able to construct a usable mental map. Do not dump a map description all at once. Reveal geography as Ira follows directions, loses sight of landmarks, notices changes in elevation, hears trains through the embankment, crosses a loading route, or chooses a longer road because a lane is a dead end.

Kuoh should feel inhabited independently of Ira. Delivery workers, shopkeepers, commuters, customers, cyclists, schoolchildren, and residents have schedules and problems of their own. Do not make every person stare at Ira or react meaningfully to his presence.

Use sound with spatial purpose: a train's low vibration travelling through pavement, a delivery van reversing between buildings, the change in footstep acoustics under an underpass, street noise muffled inside the café. Use light and materials where they clarify the physical environment: cloudy daylight under a translucent arcade roof, concrete canal banks, worn shop counters, metal shutters, loading-bay dust. Avoid decorative sensory overload.

The town's structure should help Ira learn routes. The railway embankment is a stable reference point; the roads slope toward the canal and industrial facilities; the station anchors several commercial streets. As he walks, he gradually stops needing to check every intersection. This is a small acquired competence, not proof of extraordinary intelligence.

## Movement IV — The first refusals: distinguish the answer from the story he adds

At a stationery shop's noticeboard near the station, Ira sees several temporary-work notices. Some are already filled or crossed out; others have schedules or requirements that do not suit him. One warehouse position requires a local guarantor. An evening restaurant shift may conflict with his unknown schedule after the academy result.

He reads the actual conditions. He does not pretend all options are equally viable. Let the facts exclude some opportunities without turning each into a personal humiliation.

He asks at a small repair/electrical-supplies shop. The worker is busy with stock or cables. The answer is straightforward: no temporary worker is needed today; perhaps stock arrivals next month may change that. The man may suggest a storage yard by the canal and give useful directions. He is not a mentor. He has answered a practical question.

At the storage yard, the office worker explains that the regular crew is sufficient and that a local contact is required for insurance. The refusal is based on policy, not Ira's worth. The worker has other work and returns to it.

After one or more refusals, Ira notices that his mind is already trying to generalize: “not today” becomes “not here,” then “nowhere.” He begins inventing a verdict about himself that nobody has delivered.

Do not immediately repeat the lesson in a second polished paragraph. Let the next action demonstrate that he catches the pattern: he adjusts his coat, follows the next route, or asks at another business despite the earlier result.

His previous online/social experiences can surface briefly here. He learned that conversations could become hostile, words could be detached from context, and leaving early could prevent another unpleasant exchange. The old strategy had some practical value. Its cost is that he now sometimes withdraws before anything has actually occurred. Keep the memory compact, concrete, and relevant. Do not invent a detailed incident or claim his mother is dead. His mother was alive in the previous life, as established in earlier chapters.

The narrator must not deliver a full psychological essay while walking beside the canal. One memory, one recognition, and a change in his next action are enough.

## Movement V — The canal, industrial routes, and the temptation to quit

The route follows a real physical chain: main road, railway embankment, underpass, lower commercial district, canal road, storage yard, and the industrial facility or batching plant. The route should remain consistent and comprehensible.

The canal has concrete banks and dark water, service roads, maintenance access points, gates, and occasional bridges. The industrial area contains loading bays, metal containers, equipment, delivery vehicles, and workers who are busy with actual jobs. Do not render the district as an ominous wasteland or an empty cinematic set.

Ira considers other businesses he might ask. The problem is not that there are no possible actions. The problem is that the lack of guaranteed success begins to feel like a reason to stop testing them.

This is where the prior-life pattern can connect to the present. Ira notices that he is about to reject the next attempt himself, before another person has answered. The realization should not instantly make him feel brave. It should make him continue toward the next district.

A cyclist or delivery rider can pass, forcing Ira to move aside or briefly redirect his attention. The physical world continues; the rider is not a symbol or a messenger.

## Movement VI — The covered arcade and Kissa Minami

The covered shopping passage is a recognisable place, not a generic “busy market.” A translucent roof diffuses daylight. The entrance may contain a vegetable shop or other small business. A woman sweeping outside can answer Ira's question about where temporary work might be available. She points him toward the café near the end of the arcade and explains the route opposite the bicycle repair shop.

The woman should speak naturally and efficiently. She has a shop to maintain. Her directions should be specific enough to follow and should help orient the reader.

Kissa Minami has a broad divided window, dark wooden frames, a modest sign, small tables, a counter, a ledger, cups, and the smell of coffee or food. The street noise becomes quieter when Ira enters. The proprietor is a working business owner, not a wise mentor waiting to impart the chapter's theme.

Ira has visited before, around the time of the examination. The proprietress remembers him as the boy who came in with an examination bag. Keep the recognition modest and plausible. She may ask how the examination went; he says he will not know until Friday.

Ira asks about temporary work. She explains that her own staffing is sufficient and that small businesses often hire when they need someone rather than advertising a permanent vacancy. She may point him toward the canal-side businesses or tell him to ask the people handling deliveries, who know when extra hands are needed.

Their exchange must remain grounded in the actual business context. She is not obliged to hire him, praise him, comfort him, or become emotionally invested. Her directness is not cruelty.

A short exchange about the postman and the Friday result may occur, but avoid turning it into a neat lesson about patience. The point is practical: the post arrives when it arrives.

Ira notices that she remembers him and gives directions without requiring him to become more than a customer. This can offer a small social counterexample to his tendency to assume every interaction is a verdict, but he should not overstate its meaning.

He eats something simple and continues the search. Do not let the café become a place where all the chapter's thinking is explained aloud.

## Movement VII — Learn the town by moving through it

Ira walks through smaller streets, asks at additional shops, takes a wrong turn into a service yard, retraces his route, and gradually learns the town's structure. This is not filler. The reader should feel the difference between travelling a route by rote and understanding how the streets connect.

Use compression for routine walking. Expand where a spatial change affects Ira's understanding or decision. A wrong turn may cost three minutes and reveal a route boundary; it does not need a paragraph of emotional reflection.

He asks at a hardware shop, a stationery shop, or other appropriate businesses. Answers vary: stock already delivered, no need for help, closed for the afternoon, fixed schedules already assigned. Avoid making every refusal use identical wording. Avoid presenting the whole sequence as a comic montage of rejection.

The accumulated effect is a practical fact: he has not found a job. He has spent little, learned more about the district, and continued asking. These are distinct outcomes. Do not pretend the day has been a great success.

At some point, he notices a delivery truck or medium-sized freight vehicle beside a service entrance. The truck's paperwork includes Kuoh Academy's crest, a stylized pair of wings surrounding a sword and an open book. The driver and workers continue handling crates or checking a routing sheet.

Ira notices the crest because he recognises it from the academy. It is mildly unexpected, but entirely plausible that a school receives deliveries. He does not interrupt the workers or investigate the sheet. He does not infer a supernatural conspiracy, hidden jurisdiction, sinister supply chain, or major clue. He keeps moving.

The crest is a quiet continuity marker and a piece of ordinary institutional geography. Do not make it the centre of the chapter.

## Movement VIII — Kenji: a practical connection, not instant friendship

Later, near a small grocery or similar business by the railway underpass, Ira encounters **Kenji**, a delivery worker he has previously noticed at Kissa Minami. Kenji carries flattened cardboard boxes or handles a routine delivery. His work clothes show ordinary use and dust. The encounter begins because they recognise the local context, not because fate has arranged a dramatic meeting.

Kenji knows that Ira has been asking Minami-san about work because the proprietress mentioned it. He introduces himself and explains that he makes local deliveries for several shops: equipment, supplies, and routine stock.

Their first exchange should be short and practical. Kenji may suggest asking businesses that receive regular deliveries, since they sometimes need help carrying goods. Ira has already tried several places. Kenji's response should be matter-of-fact, not a speech about perseverance.

Kenji is not Ira's instant best friend, saviour, mentor, or recruiter for a grand adventure. Their connection begins through ordinary local work and a modest referral from a business owner. There is enough social basis for Kenji to speak with him, but not enough for deep trust or personal disclosure.

Kenji is competent at his job but not omniscient. He has a route, deliveries, a depot schedule, and constraints. His dialogue should sound like someone fitting a small errand into a workday.

He asks whether Ira is busy the next morning and explains a limited delivery task for Kissa Minami: collect heating coils or a comparable clearly specified part from a supplier in the outer district and bring them to the café before lunch. The source draft uses heating coils for the café's roaster. Preserve that concrete detail unless another supplied source proves it wrong.

The task exists because Kenji's route goes in the opposite direction and rearranging the schedule risks delaying the other deliveries. Minami needs the part to complete a repair. This is a real practical reason to delegate the collection.

The errand's terms:
- Pay: **¥500**, plus the fare if a bus is needed.
- The collection is prepaid. Ira does not need to buy the part.
- The order slip will contain the supplier's address and details.
- If the supplier cannot provide the part, Ira should call the number on the slip, inform Kenji, and not waste the entire morning waiting.
- Kenji will collect the final paperwork from the depot in the evening.
- If Ira accepts, he must meet Kenji at **Kissa Minami at 9:00 the following morning** to receive the order slip, route information, and bus-stop details.
- The supplier opens at 8:30. The journey from the station is approximately twenty minutes by bus, depending on traffic. Do not invent an exact street address, bus fare, route number, or supplier name.
- Kenji needs to know Ira's decision that evening so he can tell Minami-san and organise the route.

Ira asks reasonable questions: what the item is, how far the supplier is, whether the collection is prepaid, what to do if the part is unavailable, and what the pay is. These questions should emerge naturally, not as a checklist spoken for the reader's benefit.

Ira considers real risks: misunderstanding the route, a delayed bus, the supplier not having the part, or Kenji no longer needing him. He also recognises that these are possibilities rather than evidence that the offer is unsuitable. The terms are limited enough for him to assess.

Do not let Ira accept because Kenji praises his intelligence or recognises hidden potential. Kenji offers the task because Ira is available, has asked for work, and can plausibly perform a straightforward collection. That is enough.

Do not make the offer sound suspicious without evidence. Do not make it magically solve Ira's finances. The pay is modest and the work temporary.

Kenji should leave to resume his deliveries. Their conversation ends because both have things to do. Do not force a handshake, bonding moment, life story, or declaration of trust.

## Movement IX — Confirming the errand with Minami-san

Ira returns to Kissa Minami to clarify the arrangement. He tells the proprietress that Kenji has offered a delivery and confirms the payment of ¥500 plus fare. She confirms that she is covering the expense and explains that the heating coils are needed for the roaster repair before lunch.

She reminds him to obtain the receipt so she knows the parts have been collected. This is a business requirement, not an expression of distrust or a test of Ira's character.

She asks whether he has decided. She needs to tell Kenji and organise tomorrow. Her insistence is practical, not coercive. Do not frame her as pushing him toward personal growth.

Ira sits, orders tea, and evaluates the offer. He has spent the day trying to find work and has not found a regular position. The errand is different because it is bounded: defined item, defined supplier, defined destination, prepaid collection, clear fallback instructions, limited pay, and a concrete meeting time.

His mind can still imagine ways it might go wrong. The meaningful change is that he does not treat the existence of uncertainty as sufficient reason to refuse. He evaluates the actual conditions instead.

Do not restate this decision logic repeatedly in several paragraphs. The questions he asks, the terms he confirms, and the final decision should already establish it.

He tells Minami-san that he will take the delivery. She tells him to be at the café at nine and not to be late. She returns to her ledger or work. There is no applause, praise, special treatment, or sudden warmth. The world keeps its scale.

# 11. FAILURE MODES THAT REQUIRE REJECTION

Before delivering the chapter, silently reject and repair any passage that exhibits one or more of these problems:

**A. The paragraph explains what the scene already shows.**  
Cut the redundant interpretation unless it adds a genuinely new inference.

**B. The character states a lesson too cleanly.**  
Replace the declaration with a smaller thought, action, hesitation, or decision that reflects his actual stage of development.

**C. A refusal becomes a judgment of Ira's worth.**  
Make the external reason legible. Let Ira's mind attempt to overgeneralise, then distinguish his inference from the facts.

**D. The city becomes generic scenery.**  
Tie geography to route choices, sightlines, sound, time, and the work search.

**E. The city becomes a travel itinerary.**  
Compress repeated walking and repeated business visits. Keep only encounters that contribute new information, new pressure, a change in Ira's behaviour, or a necessary transition.

**F. Every character sounds like the author.**  
Give each speaker a practical reason to say what they say. Use ordinary phrasing, interruptions, incomplete exchanges, and attention returning to work where appropriate.

**G. Kenji's offer is too convenient or emotionally loaded.**  
Ground it in the route conflict and Minami-san's need for a part. Keep the task small and the terms explicit.

**H. The chapter resolves Ira's psychology completely.**  
He makes one better decision; he does not become a different person overnight.

**I. The ending announces the theme.**  
End on the appointment, the written note, the alarm, or the light going out. Do not append a thesis statement.

**J. The prose is concise but experientially empty.**  
Do not remove physical detail that helps the reader simulate the room, route, timing, or social exchange. The goal is not maximum brevity.

**K. The prose is dense but difficult to parse.**  
Clarify who is doing what and how the physical space works. Density must come from connected information, not syntactic obstruction.

**L. An unverified detail is promoted into canon.**  
Remove or qualify it.

**M. The chapter is only an outline expanded into paragraphs.**  
Write actual scene-level fiction: specific action, embodied perception, dialogue with stakes, changes in attention, and consequences.

**N. The chapter becomes a sequence of mini-essays.**  
Keep reflection anchored to the immediate event. Do not let the narrator stop the story repeatedly to explain the same psychological pattern.
