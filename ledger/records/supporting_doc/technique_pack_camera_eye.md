# The Camera Eye — Focalization, Attention & Perceptual Access

The narrative camera is a model for controlling access, not a literal camera. It governs what the viewpoint can perceive, what they attend to, how they interpret it, and what remains outside their knowledge.

## Controls

- **Position:** where is the viewpoint relative to the action?
- **Field:** what could be perceived from there?
- **Attention:** which available details does the character notice?
- **Resolution:** broad orientation or close sensory detail?
- **Interpretation:** what does the character think the detail means?
- **Reliability:** can fear, desire, fatigue, ignorance, or memory distort that reading?
- **Access boundary:** what cannot be known yet?

A character could see something without noticing it. Attention is selective.

## Ledger case studies

**Chapter 1:** Jonah cannot move and cannot see clearly. Familiar room geometry helps him estimate where the shape is, but darkness prevents confirmation. The tension comes from confident spatial memory colliding with uncertain sight.

**Chapters 2–3:** Jonah interprets whispers and laughter through social anxiety, then catches himself: he knows what he imagines people think, not what they actually think.

**Chapter 4:** Cosmic scale is rendered through Jonah's comparisons and failures of comprehension. Do not turn his perception into an omniscient cosmology lecture.

**Chapter 6:** Ira notices bodily mechanics, station details, and institutional behavior. The shadow clue is noticed and partly rationalized, not explained.

**Chapter 8:** Ira observes social cues but overpredicts what the teenagers will ask. Observing a cue is not the same as correctly inferring intention.

## How to apply it

Define viewpoint knowledge; select details that affect the current task or emotion; choose perceptual distance; separate observation from interpretation when uncertainty matters; and reorient the reader after a close-up or disorienting beat. If narration reveals information the viewpoint cannot know, establish a source or revise it.

Wide view, medium view, close-up, and reframing can be useful planning metaphors, but they do not replace focalization.

## Failure modes

- Inventorying every object regardless of attention.
- Revealing another character's private thoughts without a narrative basis.
- Floating without spatial anchors.
- Mistaking a character's guess for confirmed fact.
- Letting visual description suppress sound, touch, smell, bodily position, or memory when those channels matter more.

## Diagnostic

Ask of each paragraph: “Why does this viewpoint notice this now?” A practical, emotional, thematic, sensory, or suspicious reason can justify it.
