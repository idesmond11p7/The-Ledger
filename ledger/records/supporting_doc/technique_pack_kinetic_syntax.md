# Kinetic Syntax — Action, Impact & Fragmented Attention

Action prose should organize the reader's perception of movement and consequence. Syntax can compress or expand a moment, but the reader still needs a coherent model of who acts, what changes, where bodies and objects are, and what follows. Fragmentation is useful when attention narrows or timing changes, not as a generic synonym for intensity.

## Action chain

1. **Initiation:** what begins the movement?
2. **Vector:** in which direction, toward what, and at what distance?
3. **Resistance:** what interrupts or redirects it?
4. **Contact:** what meets what?
5. **Consequence:** what changes in position, sensation, knowledge, or danger?
6. **Attention shift:** what does the viewpoint notice next?

Omit steps only when the reader can bridge them or when disorientation is intentional.

## Ledger cases

- **Chapter 3:** phone theft leads to pursuit; abnormal rain and a huge black vehicle intrude; impact breaks into sound, light, pain, and memory. “Tomorrow” recurs as attention slips from present action. The cause of the weather and vehicle is unresolved.
- **Chapter 1:** failed attempts to move form action beats without successful movement; sensory evidence around the immobile body escalates.
- **Chapter 7:** examination actions are procedural and restrained, proving that not all action needs rapid fragments.

## Procedure

Map the physical sequence in plain language first. Mark where attention or timing changes. Adjust sentence span and paragraph breaks around that shift. Use sound effects only when they add timing, texture, or viewpoint. Let fragments remove low-priority connective tissue, not essential cause and effect. Reorient after disorientation with a body, object, direction, sound, or consequence. Read without the onomatopoeia, then add only what earns its place.

## Failure modes

Sound effects carry the action instead of verbs; every movement gets a paragraph; repetition continues past its function; the reader cannot reconstruct whether an impact preceded a turn; memory flashes have no relation to present pressure; narration claims precise neurological states without evidence.

## Diagnostic

Ask a reader to reconstruct the sequence without your intended interpretation. If they cannot identify the main action, decide whether that confusion is desired. Otherwise restore the missing physical relation while preserving the cadence.

## Research connection

Trabasso and Suh's [work on explanatory coherence](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539309544827) is relevant to how readers connect events; it does not validate any specific onomatopoeic style.
